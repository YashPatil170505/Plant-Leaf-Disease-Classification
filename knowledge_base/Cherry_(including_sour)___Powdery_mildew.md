## Symptoms
Early symptoms are light green or light, roughly circular patches on young leaves, with a subtle white, cotton-like or powdery fungal growth [S1][S2][S4]. Infection often appears first on the leaf underside and on root suckers, low branches, the canopy interior and tree crotches where humidity is high [S1][S2]. Severely infected leaves may curl, pucker, blister or twist, and new shoot leaves can become small, pale and distorted [S1][S2][S4]. Young shoots can become covered with the fungus and may be deformed and stunted [S4]. Small black overwintering bodies (chasmothecia) form on older infected leaves [S1]. On fruit, slightly depressed circular areas appear late in the season, often starting at the stem end, with or without visible white growth [S1][S4]. Fruit covered with white fungal growth is unmarketable [S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
Cherry powdery mildew is caused by the fungus Podosphaera cerasi, formerly called Podosphaera clandestina [S1][S3]. It is an obligate parasite that grows only on living host tissue [S2]. The fungus overwinters as chasmothecia on dead leaves, in tree crotches and in bark crevices, and also in buds on twigs [S1][S3]. Rain or irrigation in spring triggers release of ascospores, which wind carries to young leaves to start primary infections [S1][S2]. These colonies then produce asexual spores that drive secondary spread to foliage and fruit, and under favorable conditions a new generation can be produced in as little as 4 to 5 days [S1][S2]. Young fruit is less susceptible than mature fruit [S1].

## Favorable conditions
High humidity and temperatures of about 70 to 80 F favor the disease [S1][S2][S3]. Free water on leaves is not required for infection [S2]. Primary infections typically follow about 1/10 inch of rain or irrigation at temperatures of 50 F or higher [S2]. Warm, dry days with morning fog or dew are ideal for rapid disease increase [S2]. Severe infections in commercial orchards occur in years with low rainfall, high humidity and warm temperatures [S3]. Vigorous growth and dense foliage also favor the disease [S1]. Most sweet and sour cherries are susceptible, and Bing is among the most severely affected cultivars [S1].

## Prevention and cultural control
Removing root suckers is important because they are highly susceptible, stay humid near irrigation and can maintain inoculum all season [S1][S2]. Pruning to open the canopy improves airflow, speeds leaf drying and improves spray coverage [S1][S2]. Irrigation should keep water off leaves and fruit, and delaying the first irrigation in dry springs can delay the onset of the epidemic [S1][S2]. Moderating shoot growth by managing nitrogen helps, since heavy nitrogen promotes susceptible growth [S1][S4]. Home growers can also plant in full sun, rake up fallen leaves and prune out severely infected shoots [S4]. Cultivars such as Lambert, Regina and Black Tartarian have moderate resistance [S1].

## Chemical and biological control
Fungicides work best when applied preventively, with a consistent program from shuck fall or petal fall through harvest [S1][S2][S3]. Product groups used include DMI (FRAC 3), QoI (FRAC 11), SDHI (FRAC 7) and quinoxyfen (FRAC 13) fungicides, as well as sulfur, lime sulfur, horticultural oils and potassium bicarbonate [S1][S3]. Resistance to FRAC 3 and FRAC 11 fungicides has been documented, so modes of action should be rotated or tank-mixed and each group used only a limited number of times per season [S1][S2][S3]. Sulfur should not be applied close to an oil spray [S1][S3]. Bacillus-based biological products are listed for use alongside cultural practices and fungicides, though their efficacy can be inconsistent [S1]. Product choice, rates and timing must follow the product label and local extension recommendations [S2][S4].

## Similar-looking problems
To confirm cherry powdery mildew, look for white, powdery or cottony fungal growth on young leaves, especially on leaf undersides, root suckers and the canopy interior [S1][S2]. Viewing leaves with the sun behind you makes sparse early colonies easier to see [S1]. Early fruit infections are hard to see, and a hand lens is needed to spot fungal growth where the stem joins the fruit [S2]. Small black chasmothecia on older leaves late in the season are another sign of the disease [S1]. Fruit lesions may appear only as slightly depressed circular areas without obvious white growth, so they should be examined closely [S1][S4].
