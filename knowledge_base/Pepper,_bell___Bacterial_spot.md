## Symptoms
Water-soaked spots appear on the lower leaf surface within 3 to 4 days of infection [S1]. Irregularly shaped, water-soaked leaf spots turn brown or brown-black [S2]. On the upper leaf surface, lesions turn tan to brown and look slightly greasy [S1]. Lesions can merge into large yellow and brown or dead areas, and holes may form as the centers drop out [S1][S2]. Infected leaves often drop early, and the resulting thinning of the canopy increases sun scald on fruit [S1]. Stems and petioles can show dark streaks and patches [S2]. Fruit lesions start as small, raised blemishes that enlarge, turn brown to black, and become raised warts or scabs [S1]. Fruit spots are often at the stem end where water collects [S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
Bacterial spot of pepper is caused mainly by Xanthomonas euvesicatoria, the predominant species on pepper in the United States, while X. perforans and X. gardneri are considered minor on pepper [S1]. The Pacific Northwest handbook names the pathogen as Xanthomonas vesicatoria, formerly X. campestris pv. vesicatoria [S2]. Pepper strains of X. euvesicatoria are divided into eleven races, numbered 0 through 10 [S1]. The bacteria are seedborne and frequently arrive with transplants [S1][S2]. They survive on volunteer plants, in soil with undecomposed crop residue, on weeds such as nightshade, and on the surface of nonhost plants [S1][S2]. Spread occurs by splashing water, rain, aerosols, and mechanical contact from pickers or equipment, especially when plants are wet [S1][S2]. Bacteria enter through wounds and natural openings such as stomata [S1].

## Favorable conditions
Warm weather, frequent wind-driven rain, and night temperatures above 65 degrees F favor bacterial spot [S1]. Wet conditions or high relative humidity combined with warm temperatures of about 75 to 86 degrees F favor disease development [S2]. Rain or heavy dew provides the water droplets that carry the bacteria between plants [S1]. In southern Florida, the most severe outbreaks occur from August through November during the rainy season [S1]. Overhead irrigation favors spread, so drip or furrow irrigation is preferred [S1][S2]. Working in fields when plants are wet increases mechanical spread [S1][S2]. Unnecessary magnesium applications may increase disease severity [S1]. Infected seed and transplants can introduce the disease at the start of the season [S1][S2].

## Prevention and cultural control
Use resistant varieties, certified disease-free seed, and healthy transplants [S1]. Resistance genes in pepper include the dominant genes Bs1, Bs2, Bs3, Bs4C, Bs7, and BsT and the recessive genes bs5 and bs6, and some newer bell varieties resist all known races [S1]. Scout fields and transplant houses frequently [S1]. Destroy volunteers and host crop debris before new plantings, and incorporate crop residue after harvest to speed breakdown [S1][S2]. Rotate out of solanaceous crops for at least two years, or three years for better control of other diseases [S2]. Use drip or furrow irrigation instead of overhead irrigation [S2]. Keep workers and equipment out of fields when plants are wet, disinfect hand tools, and locate cull piles far from field operations [S1].

## Chemical and biological control
Copper products are widely used, but copper tolerance is widespread among strains, which limits their effectiveness [S1]. Combining copper with a dithiocarbamate fungicide such as mancozeb increases the bactericidal activity of copper [S1]. Copper is used during wet periods, with caution to avoid copper buildup in soil [S2]. A calcium hypochlorite seed treatment is labeled in Washington only [S2]. The plant activator acibenzolar-S-methyl is registered in pepper only for hot or non-bell types and should be tank-mixed with another chemistry [S1]. Biopesticides are useful mainly as add-ons to copper-mancozeb programs, and their efficacy in the Pacific Northwest is unknown [S1][S2]. Rotate products with different modes of action [S1]. Product choice and rates must follow the label and local extension recommendations [S1][S2].

## Similar-looking problems
The fetched sources do not describe specific look-alike diseases for bacterial spot of pepper [S1][S2]. To confirm the diagnosis, check for water-soaked spots that first appear on the lower leaf surface and later look greasy and tan to brown on the upper surface [S1]. Check fruit for raised, brown to black scab-like lesions, especially at the stem end where water collects [S1][S2]. Look for dark streaks on stems and petioles [S2]. A history of infected seed or transplants, warm wet weather, and overhead irrigation also supports the diagnosis [S1][S2]. Because pepper strains are divided into races and copper tolerance is widespread, some resistant varieties and copper programs may not work against every strain present [S1].
