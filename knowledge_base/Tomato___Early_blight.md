## Symptoms
Early blight produces small black or brown leaf spots about 0.25 to 0.5 inch in diameter that are leathery and often show a concentric ring pattern [S1]. The spots usually appear first on older leaves [S1]. UConn describes dark brown spots that start small and enlarge, usually on the lower leaves first, with concentric "tree ring" markings [S2]. Stems develop dark, sunken, more elongated lesions that also show concentric rings, and infection of a seedling stem can girdle and kill it [S2]. On fruit, the disease causes sunken, dry spots that may have a concentric pattern and often occur near the calyx end [S1]. Fruit lesions are dark and sunken with concentric rings [S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
Early blight is caused by the fungus Alternaria solani [S1]. Its hosts include tomato, pepper and eggplant in the Solanaceae family [S2]. The fungus survives in soil on residue from infected tomatoes, potatoes and nightshade weeds [S1]. UConn notes that it also overwinters in infected seed and in plant debris from previous years [S2]. Spores are spread by wind or water splash [S1], and UConn adds rain splash of soil onto leaves, insects and animals as means of spread [S2]. Spore germination and infection require free moisture, and leaf wetness is required for spores to germinate [S1][S2]. Disease development stops in dry, hot weather [S1].

## Favorable conditions
Warm temperatures, humid weather with heavy dew or rain, and extended leaf wetness favor early blight [S2]. UConn gives a favorable temperature range of 59 to 80 degrees F [S2]. In California, the disease is uncommon and occurs mainly in coastal areas on tomatoes exposed to rain [S1]. Damage can occur when conditions remain cool and humid for several days after a rain [S1]. Because free moisture is required for infection, rain, dew and irrigation that keep foliage wet create the main infection periods [S1][S2]. The disease stops developing when the weather turns dry and hot [S1].

## Prevention and cultural control
Destroying nightshade weeds and volunteer tomato and potato plants removes sources of the fungus [S1]. Crop rotation allows infected plant debris to decompose before tomatoes are grown in the same place again [S1]. Hot water seed treatment is recommended to address seed-borne inoculum [S2]. Variety selection is part of the UConn management plan, although most California varieties are susceptible [S1][S2]. Since the pathogen also persists in plant debris in soil, removing old crop residue limits carryover to the next season [S1][S2]. In areas where the disease is uncommon, such as most of California, treatment is rarely needed [S1].

## Chemical and biological control
Protectant fungicides containing chlorothalonil, mancozeb or copper are listed for early blight, typically applied on a 7 to 10 day schedule [S2]. UC IPM lists chlorothalonil, mancozeb and fixed copper, all multi-site contact fungicides, and advises applying a fungicide when conditions favor disease and the first symptoms appear [S1]. Fixed copper is acceptable for organic production, but not all copper products are approved, so labels must be checked [S1]. The biological fungicide Bacillus subtilis is listed for suppression, starting when plants are 4 to 6 inches tall [S1]. To limit resistance, UC IPM recommends rotating fungicide groups rather than making repeated consecutive applications of the same group [S1]. Product choice and rates must follow the label and local extension recommendations, and spot-testing before widespread use helps avoid phytotoxicity [S2].

## Similar-looking problems
Septoria leaf spot can be confused with early blight, but its lesions are smaller and lack concentric rings [S2]. Bacterial spot produces very small spots, about 1/16 inch, with a water-soaked appearance [S2]. Target spot can also be confused with early blight, because target spot lesions develop light brown to gray centers with dark concentric rings and yellowing [S3]. To confirm early blight, look for larger leathery spots with concentric rings starting on older leaves, and for dark sunken lesions with rings near the calyx end of fruit [S1][S2].
