## Symptoms
Cucurbit powdery mildew causes white, powdery fungal growth on leaves, stems, and petioles [S1]. White powdery spots appear on leaf surfaces as temperatures start to rise, and they can spread until the whole leaf is covered [S2]. The powder consists of the fungus's mycelium and spores [S2]. The disease usually starts on older or shaded leaves [S1]. On cucurbits such as watermelon, early symptoms on older leaves can include a bronzed, reddish-brown discoloration before white growth appears [S3]. Affected leaves may drop early, and fruit yield and quality can suffer [S1]. Some fruit may show speckling caused by plant stress [S1]. The disease can spread quickly, covering foliage in less than 20 days [S3].

## Cause and disease cycle
Cucurbit powdery mildew is caused by the fungi Podosphaera xanthii and Erysiphe cichoracearum [S1]. Utah State lists the two pathogens as Podosphaera xanthii and Golovinomyces chicoracearum, with P. xanthii being the most common [S2]. Podosphaera xanthii is an obligate biotroph that can only survive on living host tissue [S3]. The fungus can overwinter in fruiting structures on plant debris, and airborne spores formed in late spring start new infections [S2]. Infected tissue produces more spores that wind carries to new plants, so the cycle repeats all summer [S2]. Conidia are carried by wind over long distances [S1][S3]. The fungus can also survive between seasons on weed hosts and sometimes in greenhouses [S3]. Worker and plant movement in fields can also spread spores [S3].

## Favorable conditions
Powdery mildew favors moderate temperatures of 68 to 80 degrees F, medium to high humidity of 50 to 90 percent, dense foliage, and low light [S1]. Infection can occur at relative humidity as low as 50 percent [S1]. Infection needs at least two hours of high humidity or dew on leaves, after which the disease does well in dry conditions [S2]. In contrast to many fungi, powdery mildew does not thrive with rainfall [S2]. Under moderate temperatures and high humidity, new conidia can form within a few days [S3]. Shaded and older leaves are affected first [S1]. Weeds and volunteer cucurbits nearby can harbor the fungus and raise disease risk [S1][S3].

## Prevention and cultural control
Plant resistant or tolerant cultivars, and several resistant varieties are available for squash, cucumber, and pumpkin [S1][S2]. Space plants adequately to improve airflow and reduce humidity in the canopy [S1][S2]. Remove debris, weeds, and volunteer plants that may harbor the pathogen [S1][S3]. Remove or plow under infected plant material after harvest to prevent overwintering [S2]. Scout fields weekly for new lesions starting in late spring so that control can begin before the disease spreads [S2]. Frequent, thorough scouting allows early detection [S3].

## Chemical and biological control
Conventional growers apply fungicides on a regular interval before disease appears and alternate products with different modes of action to avoid resistant strains [S1]. Fungicides work best when applied before the epidemic reaches 10 to 25 percent severity [S3]. Sulfur products work very well when applied at the first white spots, but sulfur should not be applied above 90 degrees F because it can injure foliage [S2]. Once leaves are covered with powdery mildew, chemical control is no longer effective [S2]. Active ingredients mentioned include chlorothalonil, myclobutanil, pyraclostrobin plus boscalid, quinoxyfen, metrafenone, cyflufenamid, and azoxystrobin plus difenoconazole [S1]. Fixed copper formulations are the only OMRI-listed options with some efficacy for organic growers [S1]. Podosphaera xanthii is considered high risk for fungicide resistance, so rotation and tank mixtures are recommended [S3]. Product choice and rates must follow the label and local extension recommendations [S1][S3].

## Similar-looking problems
On cucurbits, viral infections, chemical phytotoxicity, and certain nutrient deficiencies can cause early symptoms similar to powdery mildew [S3]. Microscopic examination may be needed to confirm the disease [S3]. Because powdery mildew can spread to cover foliage and fruit in less than 20 days, early detection through frequent scouting is important [S3]. The white powdery patches on leaves, petioles, and stems are diagnostic once they appear [S1][S3]. Because the disease usually starts on older or shaded leaves, those leaves should be checked first when scouting [S1]. Growers can contact their local extension office for further guidance on diagnosis and management [S3].
