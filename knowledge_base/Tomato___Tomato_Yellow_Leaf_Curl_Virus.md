## Symptoms
TYLCV causes interveinal and marginal yellowing (chlorosis) on young leaves, crumpled leaves, and upward and downward leaf curling [S1]. Infected plants are stunted and have a bushy appearance because of shortened internodes [S1]. Clemson describes upward curling of leaves, yellow leaf margins, smaller than normal leaves, plant stunting and flower drop [S3]. UF/IFAS likewise lists stunting, reduced leaf size and bright yellow leaf margins [S2]. Flowers drop before fruit set, and yield can be reduced so severely that young plants produce no fruit [S1]. The earlier a plant is infected, the greater the yield loss [S2]. Symptoms can take up to three weeks to appear after infection, and infected plants may appear randomly through a planting [S1][S3].

## Cause and disease cycle
The disease is caused by Tomato yellow leaf curl virus, a Begomovirus in the family Geminiviridae [S1]. It is transmitted by adult whiteflies, Bemisia tabaci [S1]. UF/IFAS describes transmission as persistent and circulative, so a whitefly that has acquired the virus can pass it to new plants for the rest of its life [S2]. NC State reports that a whitefly acquires the virus after 15 to 30 minutes of feeding and, after a latent period of about 6 hours, can transmit it to a new plant [S1]. Only adult whiteflies spread the virus, and adults can travel at least 10 miles [S1]. The virus is not seed-borne, but infected symptomless transplants can carry it over long distances [S1][S3]. Hosts include pepper, eggplant, potato, tobacco, jimsonweed, common bean, petunia and lisianthus, and symptomless hosts can act as reservoirs [S1]. Whiteflies may bring the virus from nearby infected weeds such as nightshades and jimsonweed [S3].

## Favorable conditions
The threat from TYLCV depends on whitefly populations, which are intolerant of winter temperatures, so the virus is less of a problem in cool climates [S1]. High whitefly populations in mature field tomatoes can serve as a source of virus for nearby younger plantings [S1]. Whiteflies have a broad host range, and crops such as cabbage and potato can support whitefly populations that move into more vulnerable crops [S2]. Harvested tomato fields that are not promptly destroyed remain a source of whiteflies and virus [S2]. Snap beans, peppers and some weeds are additional TYLCV hosts in Florida [S2]. Infected weeds near gardens can also act as sources for whiteflies [S3].

## Prevention and cultural control
Promptly destroying harvested tomato fields is the most effective way to reduce whitefly numbers and virus inoculum [S2]. Growers should use virus-free transplants and, in North Carolina, source transplants from cooler regions [S1][S2]. Resistant or tolerant cultivars help, but tolerant varieties can still become infected and lose yield when virus pressure is high, and resistance is less effective against early-season infection [S1][S2]. Rotation with non-host crops, avoiding planting near other tomato, pepper or bean fields, and reflective or metalized mulch to repel whiteflies are recommended [S1][S2]. Greenhouses can be protected with whitefly-proof screening or fine mesh covers [S1]. Infected plants should be removed and destroyed, and whiteflies monitored with yellow sticky traps and leaf inspections [S1][S3]. Weeds in and around the garden should also be controlled [S3].

## Chemical and biological control
There are no chemical controls for viruses, so management focuses on preventing infection and controlling the whitefly vector [S3]. Removing infected plants may only slow the spread of the disease [S3]. For conventional growers, insecticides are applied starting a few weeks after planting and continued as fruit ripens, with products rotated to manage resistance [S1]. Insecticide groups used against whiteflies in Florida include neonicotinoids, sulfoxaflor, flupyradifurone, pyriproxyfen, buprofezin, pyrifluquinazon, afidopyropen, spirotetramat and cyantraniliprole [S2]. Resistance to some of these, including imidacloprid, thiamethoxam, spirotetramat and cyantraniliprole, has been documented in some populations [S2]. Insecticidal soaps, mineral oil, neem or pyrethrin and horticultural oil sprays are softer options [S1][S2][S3]. Product choice and rates must follow the label and local extension recommendations [S1][S2].

## Similar-looking problems
TYLCV symptoms can be confused with potassium, magnesium and micronutrient deficiencies such as iron, zinc, manganese and boron [S1]. Heat- or drought-related leaf curl, curling from aphid feeding and other viruses can also look similar [S1]. TYLCV is typically patchy across a field and first appears on young leaves, whereas nutrient deficiencies are usually uniform [S1]. Because symptoms overlap with these problems, confirmation through a plant disease clinic or extension specialist is recommended [S1].
