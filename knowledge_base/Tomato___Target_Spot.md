## Symptoms
Early target spot lesions on leaves are dark brown pinpoints that resemble early bacterial spot [S1]. As lesions enlarge, their centers turn light brown to gray with dark concentric rings, often surrounded by yellowing [S1]. Early signs can also appear as irregular spots smaller than 1 mm with a yellow margin, some of which enlarge to about 10 mm with ring patterns that give the disease its name [S2]. The disease starts on older leaves and moves upward, and infected leaves turn yellow, collapse and die [S2]. Lesions can merge, causing blighting and premature defoliation [S1]. Stems and petioles develop dark brown, oval or long, thin lesions [S1][S2]. Fruit show small brown, sunken flecks that enlarge into deeply pitted lesions during ripening, sometimes with an X- or star-shaped crack pattern [S1].

## Cause and disease cycle
Target spot is caused by the fungus Corynespora cassiicola, which infects more than 500 plant species, including cucumber, blueberry, papaya, cotton and sesame [S1]. The fungus produces conidia throughout the season, and these spores are spread by wind and rain [S1]. Wind-blown rain spreads spores rapidly when windy, wet weather persists for several days [S2]. The fungus persists on other crops, on remains of the previous crop, and possibly on weeds [S2]. Research has found it on 39 alternative host species, and it can survive on soybean debris for up to two years [S1]. It is very common on papaya, where it causes small angular spots that can drop out to give a shot-hole effect [S2]. Fruit can be infected while green or ripe, and fruit lesions often lead to postharvest rots [S1].

## Favorable conditions
Continuous leaf wetness of about 16 to 44 hours is the most important factor for infection [S1]. UF/IFAS reports that temperatures of 83 to 90 degrees F combined with high humidity favor the disease [S1]. Dense canopies that retain moisture favor target spot, which often begins in the inner canopy and spreads outward [S1]. Mature leaves are more susceptible than young leaves [S1]. Warm, wet weather favors the disease, and overhead irrigation promotes spore production and infection [S2]. Target spot occurs in both screen houses and fields and is a major disease in Pacific island countries [S2]. In Florida, it has become one of the most destructive foliar and fruit diseases of tomato [S1].

## Prevention and cultural control
Tomato fields should be rotated with nonsolanaceous crops, and growers should start with clean, healthy transplants [S1]. Eliminating weeds, especially solanaceous ones, volunteer plants and plant debris removes sources of the pathogen [S1]. Crops should not be planted next to older diseased crops or near papaya, and nursery seedlings with leaf spots should be discarded [S2]. During the season, removing lower branches improves airflow, and overhead irrigation should be avoided [S2]. After harvest, as much crop residue as possible should be collected and destroyed, and tomatoes should not be replanted on the same land for three years [S2]. No commercial tomato varieties are resistant to target spot, though varieties differ in susceptibility and resistance from wild relatives is being bred into tomato [S1].

## Chemical and biological control
Routine preventive fungicide applications are the primary control method, and significant losses are expected without them [S1]. Active ingredients that performed well in recent Florida trials include chlorothalonil, mancozeb, difenoconazole, flutriafol, cyprodinil, pyrimethanil, fludioxonil and the SDHI fungicides benzovindiflupyr, fluopyram and fluxapyroxad [S1]. Reduced efficacy of QoI fungicides such as azoxystrobin has been reported since 2001, and SDHI fungicides have given variable control, so resistance management is important [S1]. The Pacific Pests sheet lists chlorothalonil, copper oxychloride or mancozeb, starting when the first spots appear and spraying both leaf surfaces [S2]. Product choice, rates and intervals must follow the label and local extension or agricultural authority recommendations [S1][S2].

## Similar-looking problems
Target spot can be confused with bacterial spot, especially in early lesions, as well as with early blight and gray leaf spot [S1]. Early target spot lesions are dark brown pinpoints, while enlarged lesions have light brown to gray centers with dark concentric rings [S1]. Disease that starts in the inner canopy and causes premature loss of inner foliage is a useful clue [S1]. Pitted fruit lesions with X- or star-shaped cracks also point to target spot [S1].
