## Symptoms
Leaf scorch begins as irregular dark purple spots scattered over the upper leaf surface [S1]. The spots are small and solid purple, with no light-colored center [S3]. Spots are purplish to tan and about 1 to 5 mm across [S2]. Where many spots merge, the tissue between them turns bright red to brown [S2]. The spots darken as black fruiting bodies form in them [S1]. Leaves eventually brown, dry, and curl at the margins, so they look burned or scorched [S1][S2][S3]. Elongated, sunken purplish or reddish-brown streaks may appear on stems [S3]. Petiole lesions can girdle and kill leaves [S1]. The fungus also infects flowers and fruit, and badly spotted sepals can turn brown into dead caps that lower fruit grade [S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
Leaf scorch is caused by the fungus Diplocarpon earlianum, whose asexual stage is Marssonina fragariae [S1][S2]. It is one of the most common leaf diseases of strawberry, and its host range is limited to Fragaria species [S1]. Ascospores are released from apothecia in spring and spread by wind [S1]. Conidia are produced throughout the season in acervuli and are spread by wind and splashing rain [S1]. Dormant acervuli reactivate when moisture returns [S1]. Older and middle-aged leaves are more easily infected than young leaves [S2]. In matted row systems the pathogen can build up to damaging levels, while annual systems generally see little spring carryover [S1]. Infection can weaken plants and reduce yield in matted row systems [S1].

## Favorable conditions
Disease increase is favored by leaf wetness during warm weather of 68 to 86 degrees F [S1]. Leaf scorch is favored by long periods of leaf wetness, frequent rain, and temperatures between 60 and 77 degrees F [S2]. Older plantings of susceptible varieties are more likely to be seriously affected [S1]. Spring nitrogen applications may enhance the disease [S1]. Long wet periods from irrigation can favor infection, so irrigation should be managed to avoid them [S1]. Splashing rain spreads the spores to new leaves [S1]. Inconsistent fungicide use has been linked to occurrence of the disease [S2]. Sites with poor air drainage and little sun exposure are less suitable for matted row plantings [S1].

## Prevention and cultural control
In matted row systems, choose sites with good air drainage and sun exposure [S1]. Use resistant cultivars where they are available [S1]. Replant frequently and space plants to improve airflow [S1]. Control weeds and manage irrigation to avoid long wet periods, preferably using drip irrigation [S1]. Remove foliage and crop residue after picking or renovation to reduce inoculum [S1]. Avoid heavy spring nitrogen, since it may enhance the disease [S1]. In annual plasticulture systems, action is generally not required, although plug production facilities may need to manage the disease for appearance [S1].

## Chemical and biological control
Fungicide treatments are effective during the flowering period and during late summer and fall [S1]. Captan and other strawberry fungicides appear to reduce disease incidence [S2]. Disease occurrence has been linked to inconsistent fungicide use, so programs should be applied consistently where the disease is a problem [S2]. Current product recommendations are given in the Strawberry IPM Guide [S1]. Removing foliage and crop residue after picking or renovation reduces inoculum and complements fungicide programs [S1]. Product choice and rates must follow the label and local extension recommendations [S1].

## Similar-looking problems
Leaf scorch is easily confused with common leaf spot, caused by Mycosphaerella fragariae, because both produce purplish spots [S1][S2]. Leaf scorch spots are larger and their centers stay purple or only slowly develop small brownish centers [S2]. Common leaf spot lesions are smaller, and their centers turn tan or brown and then gray or white [S2][S3]. Leaf blight, caused by Phomopsis obscurans, forms large V-shaped lesions with a dry brown center and reddish-purple border [S3]. Angular leaf spot, caused by Xanthomonas fragariae, produces water-soaked, translucent, vein-bounded spots on the leaf underside [S3]. Powdery mildew also causes upward leaf curling and purple spots, but leaf scorch has black acervuli and no mycelium on the lower leaf surface [S1].
