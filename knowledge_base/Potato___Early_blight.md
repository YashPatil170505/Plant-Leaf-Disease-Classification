## Symptoms
Early blight lesions start as small dark brown to black spots on older, lower leaves [S1]. The disease often infects the oldest leaves first and progresses upward [S2]. Lesions enlarge to about 3/8 inch and often become angular where leaf veins restrict them [S1]. Mature lesions show a target-like pattern of dark concentric rings alternating with bands of light-tan tissue [S1]. These bulls-eye lesions are the hallmark of the disease on leaves and stems [S2]. Lesions may merge, and yellowing can surround clusters of infections [S1]. Stems and petioles develop elongated brown to black lesions [S1]. Severe infection during tuber bulking can cause early senescence and lower yield [S1]. Tubers can develop dark, sunken lesions with dry, corky tissue beneath [S1].

## Cause and disease cycle
Early blight is caused by the fungus Alternaria solani, which infects leaves, stems, and tubers [S1]. Besides potato, it also infects tomato, eggplant, and hairy nightshade [S1]. Primary inoculum is spores surviving on debris from the previous season, often in neighboring fields [S1]. Less common sources include infected seed tubers, volunteer potatoes, and alternative hosts [S1]. Infected plant debris is the main source of new spores and rapid spread [S2]. Spores are spread by wind, windblown soil, splashing rain, and irrigation water [S1]. Water, insects, animals, and machinery can also move spores [S2]. Infection rises after flowering and is fastest during tuber bulking, with multiple disease cycles per season [S1]. At harvest, spores can enter tubers through lenticels and wounds [S1].

## Favorable conditions
Alternating wet and dry periods are most favorable for spore formation, dispersal, and spread of the fungus [S1]. Infection requires high humidity or free moisture from rain, dew, or irrigation [S1]. Optimum germination and infection occur at about 68 to 86 degrees F [S1]. The University of Maine gives an optimal temperature of 82 to 86 degrees F with relative humidity of 80 percent or higher [S2]. Rain is not necessary for disease onset, since mist, fog, dew, or irrigation can supply moisture [S2]. Older, senescing leaves are favored, so early-maturing cultivars are most susceptible [S1]. Plants stressed by injury, nutrient deficiency, or insect feeding are more susceptible [S1][S2]. Early blight risk is typically elevated after canopy closure and the start of tuber bulking [S2].

## Prevention and cultural control
Rotate to nonhost crops for three to four years [S1]. Use clean or certified seed [S2]. Remove or incorporate crop residue at the end of the season [S2]. Control volunteer potatoes and susceptible weeds such as nightshades [S1][S2]. Choose later-maturing or moderately resistant cultivars, since maturity is linked to resistance [S1][S2]. Time irrigation to limit leaf wetness and let leaves dry before nightfall [S1]. Space plants for airflow and irrigate early in the day, preferably with drip irrigation [S2]. Avoid nitrogen and phosphorus deficiency and maintain good fertility [S1][S2]. Scout fields once plants reach about 12 inches, especially edges next to last year's potato fields [S1]. Kill vines two to three weeks before harvest, avoid harvest injury, and cure tubers under wound-healing conditions before storage [S1].

## Chemical and biological control
Most commercial cultivars are susceptible, so foliar fungicides are the main control tool [S1]. Protectant multisite fungicides such as mancozeb and chlorothalonil are the most common, but they provide insufficient control under high disease pressure [S1][S2]. Single-site systemic fungicides, including QoI, anilino-pyrimidine, DMI, and SDHI groups, are often needed during high-pressure periods [S1]. Fungicide timing for potatoes can be based on physiological days, with action recommended beyond roughly 300 p-days [S2]. Fungicides with the same single-site mode of action should not be applied consecutively, and modes of action should be rotated or combined [S1]. Resistance to QoI and SDHI fungicides is widespread in Alternaria solani populations [S1]. Product choice and rates must follow the label and local extension recommendations [S1][S2].

## Similar-looking problems
Brown spot, caused by small-spored Alternaria species, produces round lesions that rarely exceed 3 to 4 mm and lack concentric rings [S1]. Black dot, caused by Colletotrichum coccodes, also lacks the concentric-ring pattern [S1]. Grey mold, caused by Botrytis cinerea, can resemble merged early blight lesions [S1]. On tubers, early blight can be confused with Fusarium dry rot, which typically causes more extensive rot, cavities, and mold [S1]. To confirm early blight, look for target-like lesions with concentric rings that begin on older, lower leaves [S1][S2]. Checking whether lesions are angular and bounded by leaf veins also helps distinguish early blight from the round brown spot lesions [S1].
