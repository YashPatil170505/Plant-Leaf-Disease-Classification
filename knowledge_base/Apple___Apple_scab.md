## Symptoms
Leaf lesions begin as light, olive-green spots that become velvety as the fungus produces spores, and later darken to brown [S1]. Early leaf spots often have fuzzy or fringed edges and may start along the veins, later turning black and velvety [S2][S3]. Infected leaves may become misshapen, turn yellow and drop, and heavy infection can defoliate the tree [S2][S3]. On fruit, the disease produces black, scabby spots, and infected fruit may be stunted, cracked or misshapen [S3]. On maturing fruit, lesions turn brown and corky and often crack, and late-season infections can appear as tiny black specks known as pin-point scab [S2]. Infections that occur just before harvest may not be visible at picking but can develop in storage [S2]. Fruit lesions can also reduce storage life or lead to rots [S1].

## Cause and disease cycle
Apple scab is caused by the fungus Venturia inaequalis, which can also infect crabapple, hawthorn, mountain-ash and firethorn [S1][S2]. The fungus overwinters in infected fallen leaves, where fruiting bodies called pseudothecia form [S1][S2]. In spring, ascospores are discharged during rains from about green tip until shortly after petal fall and are carried by wind to young leaves and fruit [S1]. Lesions from these primary infections produce secondary spores (conidia) that spread by rain and wind and cause repeated infections through the season [S2][S3]. Repeated cycles can lead to premature leaf drop, reduced vigor and possibly lower return bloom [S1]. Infected leaves that fall to the ground supply inoculum for the following year [S1].

## Favorable conditions
Cool, wet weather favors apple scab, so the disease is most troublesome in temperate climates [S1]. Infection requires a period of leaf wetness at a suitable temperature, roughly 6 to 23 hours of wetness at about 42 to 75 F, with colder temperatures requiring longer wet periods [S1]. For example, about 17 hours of wetness are needed at an average of 45 F versus about 6 hours at 65 F [S2]. Frequent spring rains are the main driver of primary infection [S2]. Wet leaf surfaces are needed for both spore release and infection, and dense canopies that dry slowly make the problem worse [S3]. Symptoms usually appear about two weeks after infection under favorable conditions [S1].

## Prevention and cultural control
Planting scab-resistant cultivars is described as the best and easiest option for home growers, and cultivars such as Liberty, Freedom, Enterprise, Prima, Redfree and Jonafree are listed as resistant [S2][S3]. Some cultivars such as McIntosh, Cortland and Jonagold are highly susceptible, and strains of the fungus that overcome the main resistance gene have been reported [S1]. Raking and destroying fallen leaves in fall or early spring reduces overwintering inoculum [S2][S3]. In orchards, shredding leaf litter with a flail mower and applying urea speeds leaf decomposition [S1]. Pruning to open the canopy improves air circulation, shortens leaf wetness and improves spray penetration [S1][S3].

## Chemical and biological control
Fungicides act mainly as protectants and must be on the leaf surface before spores arrive [S2][S3]. Commercial programs rely on single-site groups such as SDHI (FRAC 7), QoI (FRAC 11) and DMI (FRAC 3) fungicides, and modes of action should be rotated or combined with a multi-site protectant to slow resistance [S1]. Captan is one of the protectant products named for home orchards [S3]. Organic options include lime-sulfur, copper and biopesticides [S1]. The critical spray period runs from bud break or green tip through about a month after petal fall, with shorter intervals during wet weather [S2][S3]. Infection-risk forecasts can help time applications [S1]. Product choice, rates and timing must follow the product label and local extension recommendations [S1][S3].

## Similar-looking problems
To confirm apple scab, check for olive-green, velvety leaf spots that later darken, often along veins, together with dark, scabby or corky lesions on fruit [S1][S3]. Scab appearing in spring after a period of cool, wet weather, especially on susceptible cultivars, supports the diagnosis [S1][S2]. The same fungus also infects crabapple, hawthorn, mountain-ash and firethorn, so similar scab lesions may be seen on these related hosts [S1][S2]. Late-season fruit infections may show only as tiny black pin-point specks, and infections just before harvest may not be visible until fruit has been in storage, so suspect fruit should be rechecked after storage [S2].
