## Symptoms
On tomato leaves, bacterial spot begins as circular, water-soaked lesions, often with a faint yellow halo, that turn dark brown to black and rarely exceed 3 mm across [S2]. On older plants, infections occur mainly on older leaves, and older spots are black, slightly raised and superficial, sometimes merging into larger blotches, especially along leaf margins [S1]. Lesions can coalesce into blighted areas with general yellowing of the foliage [S2]. Infections on seedlings may cause severe defoliation [S1]. On immature fruit, spots are at first slightly sunken and surrounded by a water-soaked halo that soon disappears, and they then enlarge, turn brown and become scabby [S1]. Fruit spots may also start as small, slightly raised blisters that darken to brown and become scab-like [S2]. Clemson describes water-soaked leaf spots and raised, scabby fruit spots on green tomatoes [S3].

## Cause and disease cycle
Bacterial spot is caused by four Xanthomonas species, X. euvesicatoria, X. gardneri, X. perforans and X. vesicatoria, and X. perforans predominates on tomato in North Carolina [S2]. UC IPM refers to the pathogen as Xanthomonas campestris pv. vesicatoria and notes that some strains attack tomato, some attack pepper, and some attack both [S1]. The bacterium persists from one season to the next in crop debris, on volunteer tomatoes and on weed hosts such as nightshade and groundcherry [S1]. It is seedborne, occurring within the seed and on the seed surface, and is spread with seed or on transplants [S1]. Outbreaks are usually traced to contaminated seed or diseased transplants, and the bacteria enter through natural openings such as stomata and hydathodes or through wounds [S2]. Secondary spread occurs through splashing rain or sprinkler water, wind-driven rain, aerosols and handling of wet plants [S1][S2].

## Favorable conditions
Infection is favored by high relative humidity and free moisture on plants, and symptoms develop rapidly at 68 degrees F (20 C) and above [S1]. NC State lists temperatures of about 75 to 86 degrees F, high rainfall and humidity, and prolonged leaf wetness as favorable conditions [S2]. Night temperatures of 61 degrees F (16 C) or below suppress disease development regardless of day temperatures [S1]. Overhead irrigation moves the bacteria, which have been recovered up to 4 m away in fields and 2 m away in greenhouses [S2]. Clemson notes that the disease spreads in wet weather through splashing water and overhead irrigation [S3]. Because the pathogen can be recovered from plants without symptoms, apparently healthy transplants may still carry it [S2].

## Prevention and cultural control
Using pathogen-free seed and disease-free transplants is the best way to avoid bacterial spot on tomato [S1]. NC State recommends certified pathogen-free seed, careful inspection of transplants, and discarding nearby trays when an outbreak occurs in the greenhouse [S2]. Seed can be treated with dilute bleach, hydrochloric acid or hot water after testing a small sample for germination effects, but such treatment should not be used on pelleted seed [S2]. Trays, benches, tools and structures should be washed and sanitized between crops, and overwatering and handling of wet seedlings should be minimized [S2]. Growers should avoid sprinkler irrigation, keep cull piles away from greenhouse and field operations, and rotate with a nonhost crop [S1]. Fields planted with tomato or pepper the previous year should be avoided [S2][S3]. Drip irrigation, removal of diseased material and removal of solanaceous weeds are also advised [S2][S3].

## Chemical and biological control
Copper bactericides give only partial control; they are protectants, so they should be applied before an infection period, starting at the first sign of disease and repeated during warm, moist weather [S1]. Copper resistance has been observed, and combining copper with mancozeb can partly overcome it [S1]. NC State also lists streptomycin sulfate, bacteriophage products and the plant resistance activator acibenzolar-S-methyl, and stresses that no products are curative, so preventive application is key [S2]. For organic production, NC State lists copper formulations and Bacillus-based biocontrols such as B. amyloliquefaciens, B. subtilis and B. mycoides [S2]. UC IPM notes that some copper formulations are acceptable for certified organic produce [S1]. Product choice, rates and intervals must follow the product label and local extension recommendations, including restricted-entry and preharvest intervals [S1].

## Similar-looking problems
Early leaflet symptoms of bacterial spot overlap with bacterial speck, early blight, gray leaf spot, target spot and Septoria leaf spot [S2]. The key diagnostic feature is bacterial streaming, in which bacteria ooze from infected tissue and are visible under a light microscope, whereas fungal lesions do not show this [S2]. Bacterial spot lesions on tomato leaves are small, rarely exceeding 3 mm, and older leaf spots are slightly raised and superficial [S1][S2]. Raised, scabby spots on green fruit are another useful clue [S1][S3]. Because early symptoms of these diseases look alike, checking for bacterial streaming is the most reliable way to confirm bacterial spot [S2].
