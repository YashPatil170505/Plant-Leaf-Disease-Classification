## Symptoms
Leaf lesions begin as small yellow spots that enlarge into spots up to about 1/4 inch wide with tan to dark brown centers and a dark brownish-red or dark tan border [S1][S2]. Tiny black fruiting bodies (pycnidia) often form in the spots, frequently in a ring near the lesion border [S1][S2]. Lesions on shoots, tendrils, cluster stems and rachises are oval, sunken and purple to black [S2]. Fruit symptoms usually appear once berries are pea-sized or larger, beginning as small brownish spots that spread over the whole berry within days [S1][S2]. Infected berries shrivel and turn into small, hard, black, raisin-like mummies that often stay attached to the cluster [S1][S2]. Different stages of infection can be seen on the same cluster [S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
Black rot is caused by the fungus Guignardia bidwellii, which can infect leaves, shoots, tendrils, cluster stems and berries [S1][S2]. The fungus overwinters in mummified berries and in lesions on canes and other infected tissues [S1][S2]. In spring, after bud break, rain releases ascospores from the overwintered mummies, and these are an important source of early-season infections [S1][S2]. Conidia from pycnidia on overwintered tissues also start infections, and new lesions produce further conidia during the season [S1][S2]. Spores need free water on susceptible tissue to germinate and infect [S1]. Only young, expanding tissue is susceptible, and fruit remains susceptible until it begins to color [S1]. Spore release continues through midsummer and then declines [S1].

## Favorable conditions
Warm, humid weather favors black rot [S2]. Infection depends on the length of the wet period and the temperature, requiring about 6 hours of leaf wetness at 80 F but about 24 hours at 50 F [S1]. At higher temperatures the required wetness increases again, to about 9 hours at 85 F and 12 hours at 90 F [S1]. New infections can occur in less than 10 hours of wetness at about 60 to 85 F [S2]. Losses can reach up to 80 percent of the crop in years with favorable weather [S1]. Online infection-period models, such as the one offered through NEWA, can help predict risky periods [S2].

## Prevention and cultural control
Sanitation is a key step, and fall cleanup may be the most important practice [S1]. Growers should remove mummies from the vines and the ground, prune out infected canes and tendrils during dormancy, and remove diseased fruit as soon as it is seen in small plantings [S1][S2]. Remaining canopy mummies can be dropped to the ground and buried [S2]. Vines should be planted in sunny sites with good air movement, with rows oriented with the prevailing winds so plants dry quickly [S1][S2]. Proper vine spacing, an open canopy and good weed control also speed drying [S1]. All cultivated grape types are susceptible, but berries become resistant about 4 to 6 weeks after bloom [S2].

## Chemical and biological control
Fungicides applied early in the season are a critical part of black rot management [S2]. Protectant fungicides must be present before an infection period, while some systemic fungicides can also act on very recent infections [S1]. Protectant-only options include mancozeb, ferbam and Bordeaux mixture, and systemic options include strobilurin and sterol-inhibiting fungicides [S1]. Key timings run from early shoot growth through bloom and continue for several weeks after bloom, until fruit begins to color or becomes resistant [S1][S2]. Growers should rotate among chemical classes [S1]. Product choice, rates and timing must follow the product label and local extension recommendations [S1][S2].

## Similar-looking problems
To confirm grape black rot, check leaf spots for tan to brown centers with a dark border and tiny black pycnidia arranged near the margin of the lesion [S1][S2]. On fruit, look for berries that turn brown and then shrivel into hard, black mummies with black fruiting bodies on the surface, often alongside sunken black lesions on shoots and cluster stems [S1][S2]. Other grape leaf diseases also cause spots, including Phomopsis cane and leaf spot, which produces tiny dark spots with yellowish margins, and leaf blight (Isariopsis leaf spot), which produces large irregular spots that turn black and brittle with age [S3]. Black rot leaf spots, in contrast, are grayish-brown with a black line margin and carry black pycnidia [S3].
