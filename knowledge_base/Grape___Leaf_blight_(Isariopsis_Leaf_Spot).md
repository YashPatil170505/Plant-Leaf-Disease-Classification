## Symptoms
Leaf blight, formerly known as Isariopsis leaf spot, produces large, irregularly shaped spots on grape leaves [S1]. The spots are dull red to brown at first and turn black and brittle as they age [S1]. In a report from Argentina, the leaf spots were circular, 2 to 8 mm across, brown to purplish brown, visible on both leaf surfaces and sometimes merging together [S3]. The disease is most common late in the season on grapes that have been poorly sprayed [S1]. It is generally considered a minor leaf spot that rarely becomes severe enough to cause yield losses [S1][S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
The disease is caused by the fungus Pseudocercospora vitis [S1][S3][S4]. Older names for the fungus include Isariopsis clavispora, Phaeoisariopsis vitis and Cercospora vitis, which is why the disease is still called Isariopsis leaf spot [S4]. Its sexual stage has been named Mycosphaerella personata [S3]. The fungus produces conidia with 5 to 10 cross-walls that measure about 25 to 90 micrometers long [S3]. Hosts include several Vitis species, including European grapevine (Vitis vinifera) and American grapevine (Vitis labrusca), as well as other members of the grape family such as Ampelopsis, Cissus and Parthenocissus [S3][S4]. In inoculation tests, leaf spot symptoms developed about 20 days after plants were sprayed with the fungus [S3].

## Favorable conditions
High humidity favors infection by Pseudocercospora vitis [S3]. In Argentina the disease was found in a humid, low-lying coastal area near a river, and the authors noted that increasing rainfall could make it a threat in other regions [S3]. Management guidance for grape leaf spots emphasizes improving air circulation to encourage drying of plant tissues [S2]. The disease is most common late in the growing season [S1]. Vineyards with poor spray coverage are more likely to show the disease [S1]. Well-managed vineyards that are sprayed for other diseases seldom have leaf spot problems [S2].

## Prevention and cultural control
Promoting good plant vigor is recommended for managing minor grape leaf spots [S2]. Increasing air circulation through pruning, shoot thinning, proper vine spacing and weed management encourages drying of plant tissues and reduces leaf spot development [S2]. Keeping areas around the vineyard free of weeds and tall grass lowers relative humidity and helps vines dry more quickly, limiting fungal infection [S1]. Pruning out and removing mummified fruit and diseased canes in early winter during dormancy is part of the recommended integrated disease management [S1]. Because the disease is mainly a problem on poorly sprayed vines late in the season, maintaining good overall disease management throughout the season is important [S1][S2].

## Chemical and biological control
Leaf spots such as this one are generally controlled by fungicides applied for the primary grape diseases, especially early-season sprays [S2]. Well-managed vineyards seldom have leaf spot diseases when fungicides are applied for other diseases [S2]. Fungicides registered to protect new grapevine growth include copper-based products, captan, myclobutanil, ferbam, mancozeb and azoxystrobin [S1]. Important spray timings include early shoot growth, just before bloom, just after bloom and fruit set [S1]. Product choice, rates and timing must follow the product label and local extension recommendations [S1][S2].

## Similar-looking problems
Several other fungi cause minor leaf spots on grape, including species of Cercospora, Septoria and Pestalotia [S2]. Other grape leaf diseases can also produce spots, such as black rot, which forms small spots with a dark margin and black pycnidia, and Phomopsis leaf spot, which forms tiny dark spots with yellowish margins [S1]. Zonate leaf spot produces large circular lesions with concentric zones [S1]. In the Argentine report, the cause was confirmed by microscopic examination of the long, multi-celled spores and by pathogenicity tests, so laboratory examination is a reliable way to confirm the diagnosis [S3].
