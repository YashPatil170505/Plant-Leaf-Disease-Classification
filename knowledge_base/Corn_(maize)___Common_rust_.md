## Symptoms
The first signs of common rust are small chlorotic flecks on the leaf surface [S1]. These develop into powdery, brick-red pustules that are oval or elongated and about 1/8 inch long [S1]. Pustules are sparsely scattered and often appear on both the upper and lower leaf surfaces [S2]. Their color ranges from brick red to golden or cinnamon brown [S2]. Leaf tissue around the pustules may turn yellow or die, sometimes forming bands of dead tissue across the leaf [S1]. As the season progresses, pustules turn dark brown to black as the fungus produces dark teliospores [S1][S2]. Husks, leaf sheaths and stalks can also be infected [S1]. Rust pustules usually first appear in late June [S1].

## Cause and disease cycle
Common rust is caused by the fungus Puccinia sorghi [S1][S2]. The fungus does not survive Midwestern winters and instead overwinters in subtropical and tropical regions [S1][S2]. Each year, spores called urediniospores are carried long distances north on wind currents to reach corn fields [S1][S2]. Spores produced on infected corn then spread locally and over long distances, causing repeated secondary infections during the season [S2]. Each pustule can produce thousands of spores, so inoculum can build up quickly when the weather is favorable [S2]. The disease occurs every growing season but is seldom a serious concern in hybrid dent corn [S1].

## Favorable conditions
Infection requires at least six hours of leaf wetness or relative humidity at or above 95 percent [S2]. The optimal temperature range for common rust is about 61 to 77 F [S2]. High humidity, night temperatures of 65 to 70 F and moderate daytime temperatures favor disease development [S1]. Early-season infections combined with prolonged favorable weather can cause severity to rise rapidly [S2]. Younger leaf tissue is more susceptible than older tissue [S2]. The disease is generally more severe on seed corn than on commercial hybrids [S1]. Sweet corn can also suffer yield loss, with about 6 percent yield loss reported for every 10 percent of leaf area infected in Illinois sweet corn [S2].

## Prevention and cultural control
Resistant hybrids and inbreds are available and are the main means of managing common rust [S1][S2]. Breeding for rust resistance has received relatively little attention in the Midwest [S2]. Relatively few seed companies evaluate hybrid reactions to common rust, so ratings may not always be available [S2]. Planting early, so that crops are more mature when spores arrive, can reduce or avoid losses [S2]. Using shorter-season hybrids has the same benefit because younger leaf tissue is more susceptible to infection [S2].

## Chemical and biological control
Foliar fungicides are labeled for control of common rust [S1][S2]. Several labeled fungicides control rust when applied in a timely way, ideally shortly after infection begins [S2]. Applications are most likely to pay off on susceptible, high-yielding hybrids or on higher-value corn such as seed corn, white corn or popcorn [S2]. Before spraying, growers should check forecasts for continued humid, warm weather [S2]. Treatment thresholds vary by region, and studies in sweet corn suggest control is most effective when disease severity is still low [S2]. Product choice, rates and timing must follow the product label and local extension recommendations [S2].

## Similar-looking problems
Common rust is most often confused with southern rust [S2]. Common rust pustules form on both leaf surfaces, are brick red to cinnamon brown and are sparsely scattered, while southern rust pustules are mainly on the upper leaf surface, are lighter cinnamon-brown to orange and occur in dense clusters [S2]. Common rust spores are usually round, while southern rust spores are usually oblong to ellipsoid [S2]. Common rust is favored by cooler temperatures than southern rust [S2]. Because symptoms vary and both rusts can occur together, laboratory confirmation by a plant diagnostic clinic is recommended when in doubt [S2].
