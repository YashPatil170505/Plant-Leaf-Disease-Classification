## Symptoms
Judging leaf health starts with identifying the plant and understanding its normal growth and seasonal behavior [S1]. A condition that looks like a problem may simply be normal, such as an arborvitae that turns rusty-looking in winter [S1]. The first important step is to determine the identity of the plant and its requirements for healthy growth [S2]. Routine monitoring should note how many plants are affected, the pattern of damage across the planting, which plant parts are affected, and when symptoms appeared [S1]. Watching whether symptoms get worse, improve, or stay the same over time is also useful [S1]. Scouting should include looking for signs of pests such as slug trails, rodent holes, or notched leaves [S1]. Biotic diseases sometimes show physical signs of the pathogen, such as fungal growth or bacterial ooze [S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
Plant disease is explained by the disease triangle of a susceptible host, a pathogen, and a favorable environment [S3]. Time is added as a fourth factor, because the environment must stay favorable long enough after the host contacts the pathogen [S3]. Disease does not occur instantaneously [S3]. Most crops are planted as susceptible cultivars, although resistant or tolerant varieties exist for some diseases [S3]. Fungal pathogens can overwinter in a field on plant residue or in soil, which is why continuous cropping raises disease risk [S3]. Living causes of plant problems include fungi, bacteria, viruses, nematodes, insects, and mites [S2]. Biotic diseases can spread from plant to plant, often through wind-blown rain [S2].

## Favorable conditions
The environment is probably the single most limiting factor for the development of plant disease [S3]. Without a conducive environment, a plant disease will not occur [S3]. High humidity, free moisture from dew, and moderate temperatures favor disease [S3]. These conditions must last long enough for infection, lesion formation, and sporulation to happen [S3]. Most fungi do not tolerate temperature extremes [S3]. Plant diseases and insects often preferentially attack plants that are stressed by an abiotic factor [S2]. Several factors often interact, so both the primary problem and contributing factors need to be identified [S2]. Fields with residue from previous susceptible crops carry a higher risk because pathogens can overwinter there [S3].

## Prevention and cultural control
Integrated pest management uses environmentally sound methods first and treats pesticides as one option among many [S4]. Prevention stops pests from establishing by removing their food, water, and shelter or blocking their entry [S4]. Cultural controls include sanitation, proper watering and fertilizing, and choosing pest-resistant plants [S4]. Physical controls include barriers, traps, and spraying pests off plants with water [S4]. Biological control uses beneficial organisms called natural enemies to manage pests [S4]. Crop rotation and planting resistant varieties are important because fungi can overwinter in fields [S3]. Identifying the pest and learning its life cycle is the first step in choosing an effective method [S4]. Problems should be diagnosed before they are treated [S1].

## Chemical and biological control
Pesticides should be used only when nonchemical methods are not available or not sufficient, as a last resort [S4]. Before treating, confirm that the problem is serious enough to justify treatment [S4]. Choose the least toxic product that works, reduce exposure of people and pets, and protect the environment [S4]. After treatment, use preventive methods so pests do not return [S4]. Too often people spray first and ask questions later, when it should be the other way around [S1]. A diagnosis can be confirmed through a local extension office, where Master Gardeners can review samples and photos [S1]. Plant diagnostic clinics, such as the Iowa State Plant and Insect Diagnostic Clinic, diagnose plant problems and diseases [S2]. Product choice and rates must follow the label and local extension recommendations.

## Similar-looking problems
Abiotic disorders are caused by nonliving factors such as drought, sunscald, freeze or wind injury, chemical injury, nutrient deficiency, and overwatering [S2]. Their damage can look much like biotic disease [S2]. Abiotic damage often affects several plant species, while biotic diseases tend to be limited to certain species [S2]. Abiotic damage does not spread from plant to plant over time [S2]. Abiotic disorders do not show disease signs such as fungal growth or bacterial ooze [S2]. A uniform pattern of damage suggests a nonliving cause such as poor drainage, drought, or frost, while random damage may suggest pests or pathogens [S1]. Pest and disease problems often worsen over time, while environmental stress may stabilize or improve [S1]. Nutrient deficiencies can also occur together with diseases, as with tomatoes in high tunnels [S2].
