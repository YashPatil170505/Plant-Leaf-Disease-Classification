## Symptoms
Leaf mold causes pale green to yellowish spots on the upper leaf surface that may turn bright yellow or brown [S2]. The underside of the leaf develops a velvety olive-green to brown mold made of the fungus's spores [S2][S3]. Symptoms usually start on lower, older leaves and move up the plant, and affected leaves wither, die and drop [S1][S2]. Light green spots appear about seven days after infection, after which olive-green spores form and the spots become necrotic [S1]. Clemson describes yellowish spots on the upper surface with gray, velvety growth underneath [S4]. In advanced stages, blossoms can turn black and fall, and fruit can develop sunken, leathery black areas at the stem end [S2]. Eventually leaves drop and the plant can die [S3].

## Cause and disease cycle
Leaf mold is caused by the fungus Passalora fulva, formerly known as Cladosporium fulvum [S1][S3]. Its spores infect the lower leaf surface through stomata, and the clogged stomata impair gas exchange, leading to wilting and defoliation [S1]. Spores spread by wind, water splash and tools, and they can survive for 6 months to 1 year [S1][S2]. The fungus survives on infected plant debris or seed, and possibly in soil, which allows year-to-year survival in greenhouses [S1][S2]. Infected seed, tools and workers can carry it to new plants [S1]. The fungus can complete multiple generations in one season, producing new spores on the lower leaf surface within 10 to 12 days under favorable conditions [S2].

## Favorable conditions
Spores germinate when relative humidity is above 85 percent [S1]. UConn gives favorable conditions as relative humidity above 85 percent and temperatures of 68 to 77 degrees F [S2]. The pathogen is favored by high relative humidity and free moisture [S3]. Leaf mold is common in greenhouse and high tunnel tomatoes and uncommon in field tomatoes [S1][S3]. Outdoors it can still occur during prolonged leaf wetness and humidity, especially in cool, damp weather [S2]. Clemson notes that the disease appears first on older lower leaves in humid, poorly ventilated conditions [S4]. Its spread in New York high tunnels has grown as more tunnels have been built [S1].

## Prevention and cultural control
Leaf mold-resistant varieties are recommended, along with better air circulation through pruning, wider row spacing and opening vents [S3]. Cornell trials found Red Mountain and Primo Red among the most resistant determinate varieties in a high tunnel [S1]. Humidity can be reduced with greenhouse fans and ventilation, by avoiding overhead watering, and by keeping greenhouse night temperatures above outside temperatures to reduce condensation [S2]. Infected material should be removed and destroyed promptly, and tools and structures sanitized between seasons [S2][S3]. In greenhouses, Cornell advises removing diseased plants and the plants around them and sterilizing equipment [S1]. Rotation, moving high tunnels regularly and using clean seed from a reputable source also help [S1][S2]. Clemson adds removing crop residue, staking and spacing plants widely, and avoiding wetting leaves [S4].

## Chemical and biological control
Fungicides can help manage leaf mold on tomatoes [S3]. Clemson lists preventive fungicides containing chlorothalonil, mancozeb or copper [S4]. Cornell mentions conventional products containing azoxystrobin plus difenoconazole and chlorothalonil, and reports that some products approved for organic production gave good control in its 2017-2018 organic field trials [S1]. UConn treats fungicides as one part of management alongside improving air circulation, reducing humidity and sanitation [S2]. Product choice, timing and rates must follow the label and local extension recommendations, such as the Cornell guidelines or the New England Vegetable Management Guide [S1][S2]. UConn also advises spot-testing products for phytotoxicity before widespread use [S2].

## Similar-looking problems
Magnesium deficiency can produce leaf symptoms similar to leaf mold and also appears first on lower leaves, but it tends to stay in that part of the plant [S1][S2]. Leaf mold spots are more discrete than the diffuse spotting caused by nutrient deficiency [S1]. Gray mold (Botrytis) affects stems and looks fuzzier on close inspection [S2]. Early stages of other tomato diseases can also resemble leaf mold [S1]. To confirm leaf mold, check the underside of yellow spots for the olive-green to brown velvety growth [S2][S3].
