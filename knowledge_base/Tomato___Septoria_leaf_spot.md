## Symptoms
Septoria leaf spot produces small, circular lesions about 1/8 inch across with dark borders and grayish centers [S2]. The University of Wisconsin describes spots about 1/4 inch in diameter on leaves and stems with a whitish center and a dark border [S1]. Tiny black or brownish dots inside the lesions are the spore-producing structures of the fungus, called pycnidia [S2]. Symptoms first appear at the base of the plant, and infection moves up from the lower leaves [S1][S2]. Spots on a leaf can merge and destroy leaf tissue, leading to total defoliation of lower leaves and even plant death [S1]. The fungus can infect all above-ground parts, but fruit infection is rare [S2]. Losses come mainly from defoliation, which can prevent fruit from maturing and cause sunscald on exposed fruit [S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
Septoria leaf spot is caused by the fungus Septoria lycopersici [S1][S2]. Besides tomato, hosts include ground cherries and solanaceous weeds such as jimsonweed, nightshade and horsenettle [S2]. The fungus survives in plant debris or on infected plants [S1], overwintering on debris of tomato and related plants [S2]. It can be transmitted by infected seed, and spores may persist around greenhouses, cold frames and flats [S2]. Spores spread by rain splash, wind, insects and other animals, including humans [S2]. Once infection begins, the fungus produces new spores that rapidly increase spread [S2]. Spores are not produced below 59 degrees F, so early-season infection depends on overwintered spores [S2].

## Favorable conditions
Dense foliage that creates high humidity and long periods of leaf wetness favors the disease [S1]. Symptoms typically begin as plant canopies start to close [S1]. In Maine, the disease usually appears in early to mid-August, when foliage is dense enough to restrict air movement [S2]. Infection can occur when relative humidity stays at 100 percent for more than 48 hours, and these periods can accumulate over several days [S2]. The optimal temperature range is 68 to 77 degrees F [S2]. Free water on leaves dries slowly under a dense canopy, prolonging infection periods [S2]. Overmulching can keep soils wet and should be avoided [S1].

## Prevention and cultural control
Resistant tomato varieties should be used when possible, and plants should be spaced and thinned or pruned to improve airflow and shorten leaf wetness [S1]. Disease-free seed should be used, or suspect seed can be hot-water treated, which may reduce viability [S2]. Crop debris should be removed and destroyed at season's end or plowed under to speed its breakdown [S2]. Rotating to non-susceptible crops for three years and controlling susceptible weeds such as nightshade, jimsonweed and horsenettle reduce inoculum [S2]. Staking plants, maintaining adequate nutrition, hand-picking infected leaves and avoiding work in wet foliage are also recommended [S2]. Watering early in the day and avoiding wetting foliage help limit infection [S2]. A thin layer of mulch, about one inch, is suggested [S1].

## Chemical and biological control
Where the disease is chronic, preventive applications of fungicides containing copper or chlorothalonil labeled for vegetables can be considered [S1]. These should begin very early, ideally before symptoms develop, and be repeated regularly for the rest of the season, because control is difficult once symptoms appear [S1]. Maine Extension lists active ingredients including azoxystrobin, chlorothalonil, copper, mancozeb, tetraconazole and ziram, typically applied at 7 to 14 day intervals [S2]. Biological and organic-labeled options listed include Bacillus mycoides isolate J and hydrogen dioxide plus peroxyacetic acid [S2]. Tetraconazole should be applied before disease onset with no more than two consecutive applications [S2]. Product choice and rates must follow the label and local extension recommendations, including local pesticide ordinances [S1][S2].

## Similar-looking problems
Septoria leaf spot can be confused with early blight, but Septoria lesions are smaller and lack the concentric rings of early blight [S3]. Bacterial spot produces very small spots, about 1/16 inch, with a water-soaked appearance [S3]. Early leaflet symptoms of bacterial spot also overlap with Septoria leaf spot, and bacterial streaming from cut lesions under a microscope indicates bacterial spot rather than a fungal disease [S4]. To confirm Septoria leaf spot, look for small round spots with gray or whitish centers and dark borders containing tiny black pycnidia, starting on the lowest leaves [S1][S2].
