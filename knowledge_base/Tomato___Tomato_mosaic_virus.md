## Symptoms
A mosaic pattern of light green areas between yellowing veins appears on newer leaves about 10 to 14 days after infection [S1]. Leaves may curl and become pointed or fern-like, and the tissue then develops yellow streaking or spotting and can become fully chlorotic [S1]. New growth is deformed, and mottling, necrosis and stunting may occur [S1]. Some strains infect fruit, causing brown spots or blotchy discoloration on the surface and browning or mottling inside [S1]. Severe infection causes distorted fruit, delayed and uneven ripening, stunting and poor yields [S1]. Related tobamovirus infections in greenhouse tomato cause dark green and yellow leaf mottling, leaf curl and malformation, poor fruit set and small fruit [S3]. Clemson notes that the closely related tobacco mosaic virus can deform leaflets into a shoestring-like shape [S4].

## Cause and disease cycle
The disease is caused by Tomato mosaic virus (ToMV), a single-stranded, positive-sense RNA virus [S1]. ToMV is closely related to Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) but is treated as a distinct virus, and several ToMV strains cause different symptoms [S2]. The virus spreads mechanically through contaminated tools, hands, clothing, plant debris, grafting, seed and tobacco products [S1]. ToMV can be seedborne, and it is not transmitted by insect vectors [S2]. It can be carried on the mouthparts of chewing insects or animals, but there is no true insect vector [S1]. The virus is very stable and can survive at least 50 years in dead, dry plant debris [S1]. Tobamoviruses can live on seed and persist in soil for long periods [S3].

## Favorable conditions
Symptoms are more severe at 24 C than at higher or lower temperatures [S1]. Low light also promotes symptom expression [S1]. Viruses of this type spread easily whenever even a small amount of sap moves from an infected plant to a healthy one [S4]. Handling plants with contaminated hands, tools or clothing, and grafting, therefore spread the virus readily [S1]. Infected seed, plant debris, dried tobacco products and perennial weeds are sources of tobamovirus infection in greenhouses [S3]. Unlike most tomato viruses, which survive only in living plants or briefly in insects, tobamoviruses are mechanically transmitted and persist on seed and in soil [S3]. Co-infection with a mild ToMV strain and Cucumber mosaic virus can cause severe symptoms [S1].

## Prevention and cultural control
Planting resistant varieties, scouting for infected plants, and sanitation of seeds, soil, tools, workers and plants are the main recommendations [S2]. Growers should start with virus-free seed and plants because the virus spreads through propagation, grafting and seed [S1]. Seed should come from reputable sources, and untreated seed can be treated by the grower, starting with a small test lot [S3]. Transplants should be started in individual pots and handled as little as possible, and seedlings showing twisting, mosaic or unusual growth should be discarded [S3]. Tools, hands, stakes and tables should be disinfested regularly [S3]. Hands can be washed and dipped in milk after handling infected plants, and tobacco products should be kept away from plants [S1]. Weeds should be removed in and around growing areas [S1][S3].

## Chemical and biological control
There are no chemical controls for viruses [S4]. Infected plants should be removed carefully, without touching healthy plants, and destroyed to prevent spread [S1][S3]. ToMV has no true insect vector, so insecticides are not the basis of management, and control relies on sanitation, clean seed and resistant varieties [S1][S2]. Several tomato cultivars have been bred for ToMV resistance, and cross protection using a mild virus strain is another form of protection [S1]. Tobamovirus resistance levels can vary by strain [S3]. Any disinfectant or seed treatment product should be used according to its label and local extension recommendations, and bleach solutions are corrosive to metal tools [S3].

## Similar-looking problems
Herbicide damage from 2,4-D and related products causes curling, folding and twisting of foliage, vein chlorosis and irregularly shaped fruit that can resemble ToMV [S1]. Tobacco etch virus and Potato virus Y also cause leaf mosaic, curling and distortion with internal fruit browning, but they are spread by aphids [S1]. Viral symptoms can also resemble nutritional disorders [S3]. Quick immunostrip tests currently available do not distinguish ToMV from TMV [S2]. Diagnosis through a local extension agent or plant disease clinic is recommended [S3].
