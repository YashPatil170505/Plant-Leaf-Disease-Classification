## Symptoms
Infected trees may show no symptoms for a period before problems appear [S1]. Early symptoms include vein yellowing and an asymmetrical chlorosis called blotchy mottle, which is the most diagnostic symptom of the disease, especially on sweet orange [S2]. Leaves may also be small and upright [S2]. Yellowing spreads through the canopy over several years, and twig dieback is common [S2]. Fruit are few, small, often lopsided, and frequently stay green at the stylar end, and many drop before harvest [S2]. Fruit from infected trees can also be bitter and irregularly shaped, so they can only be sold for juice [S1]. Root systems are heavily damaged, with losses of 30 to 50 percent early in infection and more than 70 percent once canopy decline is visible [S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
Huanglongbing (HLB), also called citrus greening, is a bacterial infection of citrus plants [S1]. The pathogen is the bacterium Candidatus Liberibacter asiaticus (CLas), which has not been cultured, so diagnosis relies on PCR testing of symptomatic tissue [S2]. The Asian citrus psyllid (Diaphorina citri) is the insect vector that transmits the bacterium [S1][S2]. Psyllids spread the bacterium from plant to plant as they move, especially during spring and summer flushes [S2]. An infected tree can remain symptomless while serving as a source of bacteria for other trees [S1]. HLB can also spread through infected budwood, and orange jessamine and box orange can host both the psyllid and the bacterium [S2]. Moving citrus plants, branches, green waste, or fruit can carry the pest to new areas [S1]. There is no cure, and most infected trees die within a few years [S1].

## Favorable conditions
Psyllids are present in Florida year-round, and their activity peaks when new flush growth appears in spring and summer [S2]. The Asian form of the disease thrives in warm, low-altitude areas [S2]. Dry-season stress, from October to May in Florida, makes affected trees more vulnerable because their root systems are reduced [S2]. Young plantings in low-prevalence areas should be scouted at least four times a year, mainly from October to March [S2]. In the United States the disease is found throughout Georgia, Florida, Mississippi, Puerto Rico, and the U.S. Virgin Islands, and in portions of Alabama, Arizona, California, Louisiana, South Carolina, and Texas [S1]. Asymptomatic infected trees allow the bacterium to keep spreading before growers notice it [S1].

## Prevention and cultural control
Buy only USDA-certified citrus plants and check local quarantine boundaries before moving any citrus material [S1]. Planting certified, HLB-free trees is recommended, and Florida requires budwood sources and nursery production to be carried out under psyllid-proof enclosures [S2]. Removal of infected trees is the only way to ensure they will not serve as a source of the bacterium, and pruning is not successful because roots are infected before canopies show symptoms [S2]. Symptomatic trees found while scouting should be marked and mapped for removal [S2]. Windbreaks can reduce psyllid migration, and exclusion netting can protect young trees [S2]. Infected trees take up nutrients poorly, so nutrients should be applied in frequent, small, split doses guided by leaf and soil analysis, although nutrition and irrigation will not cure HLB [S2].

## Chemical and biological control
There is no treatment or cure for citrus greening, and intensive chemical control of the psyllid is the main management tool, although it is costly and increasingly ineffective [S1]. Integrated psyllid programs combine insecticides rotated by mode of action, because insecticide resistance has been found in some counties [S2]. Natural enemies such as lady beetles and the parasitoid Tamarixia radiata help suppress psyllids, but biological control alone has not slowed spread enough [S2]. Oxytetracycline trunk injection has shown promising but inconsistent results, so no formal recommendation is made for it [S2]. Product choice and rates must follow the label and local extension recommendations. HLB is federally regulated under 7 CFR 301.76, and regulated articles must not be moved out of quarantined areas [S1]. Suspected detections should be reported immediately to a State Plant Health Director, and positive samples from non-regulated areas must be confirmed by APHIS [S1].

## Similar-looking problems
HLB leaf patterns often resemble mineral deficiencies, and the small upright leaves can look like zinc, iron, or manganese deficiency [S2]. Because of this, PCR confirmation is recommended when symptoms are unclear [S2]. Other problems that can be confused with HLB include severe citrus tristeza virus, Phytophthora root rot, waterlogging, citrus blight, leafminer damage, and citrus stubborn disease [S2]. Asymmetrical, blotchy mottling of the leaves is a useful clue pointing toward HLB [S1][S2]. Checking for Asian citrus psyllids, whose nymphs leave waxy white residue and whose adults are about 1/8 inch long with mottled brown wings, can also support a suspicion of HLB [S1].
