## Symptoms
Spider mite feeding on plant sap causes fine white-to-yellow stippling, often mottled or speckled, usually noticed first on upper leaf surfaces [S1]. The mites pierce plant cells and drain their contents, leaving spotty yellowing on leaves [S2]. Damaged leaves brown, dry out and may curl, and leaf undersides can show yellowing, bronzing or a sandblasted appearance [S1]. Plants may yellow and drop leaves, exposing fruit to sunscald [S1]. Stippling first appears at the base of the plant and moves upward, and heavy infestations cause bronzing, bleaching, leaf drop, reduced growth and yield, and even plant death [S2]. At high numbers, fine webbing appears on leaf undersides and can extend to stems [S1]. High populations are almost always accompanied by webbing that contains cast skins and looks powdery [S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
The damage is caused by the two-spotted spider mite, Tetranychus urticae, a tiny arachnid about 0.5 mm long with an oval, yellowish-green body and two dark spots on its sides [S1]. It feeds on more than 1,000 plant species [S2]. Eggs are round and laid on leaf undersides or in webbing [S1][S2]. The life stages are a six-legged larva, two eight-legged nymphal stages and the adult [S2]. A full life cycle takes about 7 days to 3 weeks depending on temperature, and as little as about 5 days above 90 degrees F, with multiple generations per year [S2]. Mites overwinter as mated females in diapause under ground cover, in loose soil or under bark, and in warmer areas some remain active on other hosts [S2]. Workers and machinery brushing against infested plants can spread mites [S2].

## Favorable conditions
Spider mites prefer warm, dry conditions in which they reproduce rapidly [S1]. Reproduction is greatest when temperatures are above 80 degrees F and humidity is below 90 percent [S1]. Under ideal hot, dry conditions, populations can increase 10-fold per week [S2]. The mites are more common in protective structures such as high tunnels, which limit rain splash and raise temperatures, although they also occur in fields [S1]. A lack of natural enemies, well-fertilized plants and drought-stressed plants also favor outbreaks [S2]. Black plastic mulch, sandy soils and water-stressed weeds around field edges increase risk [S2]. Frequent use of neonicotinoid and pyrethroid insecticides can increase mite reproduction and remove predators, and broad-spectrum fungicides can reduce fungi that infect mites [S2].

## Prevention and cultural control
New plants should be inspected regularly before being brought into a growing area [S1]. Scouting requires magnification, and a hand lens of at least 10x is recommended to check leaf undersides, especially where stippling shows on the upper surface [S1][S2]. Shaking plants over white paper reveals small moving dots [S1]. For tomatoes, upper-canopy terminal leaflets should be sampled weekly, especially in hot, dry weather, with attention to field edges [S2]. Spraying plants with high-pressure water can dislodge mites [S1]. Regular watering, avoiding very hot planting sites, and managing weeds in and around fields and greenhouses from fall through winter reduce problems [S2]. Limiting other plant stresses and working low-infestation fields before heavily infested ones also helps [S2].

## Chemical and biological control
Predatory mites such as Phytoseiulus persimilis and Neoseiulus fallacis can be released, with commercial predators typically taking close to three weeks to establish [S2]. Other natural enemies include Stethorus lady beetles, lacewings, predatory thrips, predatory midges and pirate bugs [S1][S2]. The fungus Neozygites floridana is a major natural cause of mite population declines in field crops [S2]. Conventional miticides mentioned include abamectin, bifenazate and sulfur-based products, which need thorough coverage of leaf undersides [S1]. Biorational options include horticultural oils, insecticidal soaps and sulfur, which act by contact and have little residual activity [S2]. Resistance develops quickly, so modes of action should be rotated between sprays [S2]. Product choice and rates must follow the label, including crop registration and bee toxicity, and local extension recommendations [S1][S2].

## Similar-looking problems
Spider mite damage can resemble drought stress [S2]. To confirm mites, inspect leaf undersides with a hand lens for the tiny mites, their round eggs and webbing [S1][S2]. Shaking foliage over white paper and watching for small moving dark dots is another quick check [S1]. Predatory mites can be told apart from spider mites because they are pear-shaped, clear to orange, have long forelegs and lay oval rather than spherical eggs [S2].
