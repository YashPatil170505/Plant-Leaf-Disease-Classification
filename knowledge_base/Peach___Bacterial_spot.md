## Symptoms
On leaves, symptoms begin as small, water-soaked lesions that enlarge and turn reddish brown or black over time [S1]. The lesions are angular because leaf veins limit their growth [S1][S2]. The centers of the spots often fall out, leaving a reddish margin and a shot-hole appearance [S2]. Severely infected leaves turn yellow and drop, and heavy defoliation can result [S1][S2]. On fruit, small water-soaked spots can develop at any time during fruit development [S1]. Early-season fruit infections cause pitting or cracking, while later infections cause only superficial damage or a mottled look [S1][S2]. Symptoms can also develop as cankers on twigs, which carry the bacteria over winter [S1][S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
Bacterial spot is caused by the bacterium Xanthomonas arboricola pv. pruni [S1][S2]. It is an economically important disease of peach and other stone fruit, and its severity varies from year to year with the weather [S1]. The bacteria overwinter in cankers on shoots and twigs [S2]. In spring, bacteria ooze out of cankers on the previous summer's twigs [S1]. The bacteria are then spread by rain and insects to developing leaves and fruit of the current year's growth [S1]. Wind-driven rain carries the bacteria from cankers to leaves, fruit, and shoots [S2]. Wounds on leaves, such as those caused by wind-blown sand, create entry points for infection [S1].

## Favorable conditions
Wet, humid weather promotes the spread of bacterial spot [S1]. Temperatures above 75 degrees F favor spread of the pathogen [S1]. Disease severity varies by year depending on the weather, so wet seasons bring higher pressure [S1]. Sandy soils and stressed trees increase risk, because wind-blown sand can wound leaves and create entry points for the bacteria [S1]. Trees that are over-fertilized or under-fertilized are more vulnerable to bacterial spot [S2]. Vigorous trees fertilized according to recommendations are less susceptible [S1]. Planting new trees near older susceptible trees raises the chance of infection, because the older trees can carry the bacteria in their twig cankers [S2]. Susceptible cultivars such as Bounty, Caro King, and Suncrest are at greater risk than resistant ones [S1].

## Prevention and cultural control
Planting resistant varieties is the most sustainable way to control bacterial spot [S1]. Choosing peach varieties with good resistance is described as the best control option for peaches, although most apricot and nectarine varieties are susceptible [S2]. Resistant peach varieties include Augustprince, Autumnprince, Goldprince, Juneprince, Harvester, Loring, Red Haven, and Summerprince [S1]. New trees should be planted away from older susceptible trees [S2]. Pruning to control vigor and improve air movement and sunlight helps foliage dry and improves spray coverage [S1]. Growers should avoid sandy sites and tree stress where possible [S1]. A balanced fertilization program that follows recommendations helps, since both over- and under-fertilized trees are more vulnerable [S1][S2].

## Chemical and biological control
Copper sprays can be applied during the dormant period and continued through spring and summer to reduce spread and overwintering inoculum [S1]. Antibiotic sprays are also used alongside copper in commercial programs [S1]. Spray programs should run from the late dormant period through shuck split [S1]. These sprays are preventive rather than curative, so applications must be timely [S1]. Even resistant varieties may need sprays in wet years [S1]. Integrated pest management practices can often reduce the need for chemical sprays [S2]. Commercial growers should consult a current spray guide, and homeowners have fewer product options [S1]. Product choice and rates must follow the label and local extension recommendations, and the label must always be followed as required by law [S1][S2].

## Similar-looking problems
Peach scab can look similar on fruit, but it does not cause pitting and does not infect leaves [S1]. Copper spray injury can also mimic bacterial spot, but its spots are rounder and are not bounded by leaf veins [S1]. Because bacterial spot symptoms may resemble other diseases, culturing and other laboratory testing are advised to confirm the diagnosis [S2]. Bacterial ooze seen under a microscope is a sign that supports a diagnosis of bacterial spot [S2]. Checking whether leaf lesions are angular and limited by veins, whether spot centers have dropped out, and whether twig cankers are present can help separate bacterial spot from other problems [S1][S2].
