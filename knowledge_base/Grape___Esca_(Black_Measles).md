## Symptoms
The typical leaf symptom is an interveinal striping, often called a tiger stripe pattern, in which stripes start dark red on red cultivars or yellow on white cultivars and then dry and become necrotic [S1][S3]. Leaf symptoms can appear throughout the season but are most common in July and August, and they are often limited to one shoot or to shoots from the same spur or cane [S1]. On berries, small, round dark spots with a brown-purple border appear between fruit set and ripening and are easiest to see on white cultivars [S1][S3]. Severely affected berries may crack, dry out or spoil [S1]. Cross-sections of affected wood show concentric rings of dark spots [S1]. A severe form called apoplexy causes sudden dieback of shoots [S1].

## Cause and disease cycle
Esca is caused by a complex of wood-infecting fungi that includes Phaeomoniella chlamydospora and species of Phaeoacremonium, such as Phaeoacremonium aleophilum [S1][S3]. It is one of several grapevine trunk diseases, along with Eutypa, Botryosphaeria and Phomopsis dieback [S1][S2]. Spore-producing structures form in diseased wood, and spores are released during rainfall from fall through spring [S1]. Fresh dormant pruning wounds are the main infection sites [S1]. Once established, the fungi cause a permanent, localized wood infection that fungicides cannot eradicate [S1]. Symptoms usually first appear in vineyards that are 5 to 7 or more years old, although infection occurs earlier in younger vines [S1]. Symptom expression varies from year to year [S1].

## Favorable conditions
Rainfall from fall through spring favors the disease because it releases spores from infected wood [S1]. Fresh pruning wounds made during the dormant season are susceptible for several weeks, with susceptibility declining over time [S1]. Frequent rains in early winter coincide with heavy spore release, so pruning during this period increases the risk of infection [S2]. Year-to-year differences in foliar and fruit symptoms may be linked to climate-related changes in toxin production by the pathogens [S1]. The apoplexy form is reported to be more common in Europe [S1]. Every California vineyard is likely to become infected with one or more trunk diseases eventually [S2].

## Prevention and cultural control
Preventive practices are the most effective approach to trunk diseases and are most useful when started in young vineyards and applied every year [S2]. Delayed pruning, done as late in the dormant season as possible, reduces infection because wounds are less susceptible and fewer spores are released [S2]. Double pruning, a rough pre-pruning in early winter followed by final hand pruning late in the dormant season, is an option for large cordon-trained vineyards [S2]. In established vineyards, cutting out diseased wood back to healthy tissue, removing and destroying the debris, and retraining new trunks or cordons can help maintain production [S2]. Removing infected fruit before harvest is also suggested [S3].

## Chemical and biological control
No fungicide can eradicate an established esca infection in the wood, so chemical measures focus on protecting pruning wounds [S1][S3]. If delayed pruning is not feasible, or for extra protection, pruning wounds can be treated with a labeled wound protectant [S2]. A boric acid wound sealant in acrylic paint is described as effective against both Eutypa dieback and esca, and an essential-oil wound treatment is also listed [S2]. All dormant-season wounds, including larger cuts made when repositioning spurs, are vulnerable and should be protected [S2]. One extension source states that there are currently no fully effective management strategies for black measles [S3]. Product choice, rates and timing must follow the product label and local extension recommendations [S1][S2].

## Similar-looking problems
Mild esca infections can be confused with other diseases or with nutritional deficiencies [S3]. Esca is part of a trunk disease complex with Eutypa, Botryosphaeria and Phomopsis dieback, which also infect wood through pruning wounds and cause dead spurs, arms or canes [S1][S2]. To confirm esca, look for interveinal tiger-stripe leaf symptoms together with dark-spotted berries on the same shoots and dark spots in concentric rings in cross-sections of the wood that supplies symptomatic shoots [S1]. Because foliar and fruit symptoms vary from year to year, a vine that looks healthy in one season may show symptoms in another [S1].
