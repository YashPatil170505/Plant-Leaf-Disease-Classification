## Symptoms
On apple and crabapple, tiny yellow spots appear on the upper leaf surface in spring [S1][S3]. The spots enlarge and turn yellow to bright orange, often with a red border, and tiny black dots form in their centers [S1][S2]. By midsummer, small cup- or tube-like structures called aecia develop on the leaf underside directly beneath the spots [S1][S2]. On fruit, small orange, slightly raised lesions appear that later crack and turn brown, and infected fruit stems may cause early fruit drop [S2]. On the alternate host, eastern red cedar or juniper, the fungus forms brown, dimpled galls on twigs [S1][S2]. During rainy spring weather, mature galls push out bright orange, jelly-like telial horns [S1][S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
Cedar apple rust is caused by the fungus Gymnosporangium juniperi-virginianae, which must alternate between apple or crabapple and red cedar or certain junipers to complete its life cycle [S1][S2]. The full cycle takes about two years [S1][S2]. In spring, telial horns on cedar galls release basidiospores that wind carries to apple leaves and fruit [S2]. Symptoms on apple typically appear 10 to 14 days after infection [S2]. In mid to late summer, aecia on apple leaves release aeciospores that infect cedar or juniper [S1][S2]. These spores do not reinfect apple, so there is no repeating cycle on apple within a season [S2][S3]. New galls develop on cedar and mature to produce telial horns in the second spring after infection [S1][S2].

## Favorable conditions
Spring rains cause cedar galls to swell and release spores [S1][S2]. A wet period of about 4 to 6 hours at 50 to 75 F is enough for severe infection of apple [S1]. Basidiospores form at roughly 46 to 77 F, and infection can occur at temperatures as low as 34 F [S2]. Young apple leaves about four to eight days old are the most susceptible [S2][S3]. Fruit is most susceptible from tight cluster through petal fall [S2]. A film of water on the apple tissue is needed for infection [S3]. Most infections occur within several hundred feet of infected cedars, although spores can travel several miles [S1].

## Prevention and cultural control
Planting resistant apple and crabapple varieties is a primary recommendation [S1][S3]. Red Delicious, Gala Supreme, McIntosh and Liberty are listed as resistant, while Golden Delicious, Rome Beauty and York Imperial are highly susceptible [S2]. Removing galls from junipers before the telial horns form reduces spring spore production [S1][S3]. Removing nearby wild apples, crabapples, cedars and junipers can also help [S3]. Removing alternate hosts within about 2 miles of susceptible apples disrupts the life cycle and makes fungicides more effective, although complete removal is often not practical because cedars and junipers are so common [S2].

## Chemical and biological control
Fungicide sprays on apple typically begin around tight cluster and continue at intervals through about 10 to 14 days after petal fall [S2]. Sterol-inhibiting (DMI, FRAC 3) fungicides are the most effective group, while QoI (FRAC 11) and SDHI (FRAC 7) fungicides have limited efficacy [S2]. Myclobutanil is named as an option for home growers, along with Bordeaux mixture [S3]. Sprays applied for apple scab, which has a similar infection period, have historically also controlled cedar apple rust [S2]. Product choice, rates and timing must follow the product label and local extension recommendations [S2][S3].

## Similar-looking problems
Quince rust (Gymnosporangium clavipes) and hawthorn rust (Gymnosporangium globosum) produce similar symptoms on apple and are often confused with cedar apple rust [S2]. Cedar-quince rust mainly affects thorns, twigs and fruit and causes almost no leaf spots, while cedar-hawthorn rust causes yellow to orange leaf spots, sometimes with fruit and stem infection [S1]. On cedar, cedar apple rust forms round to kidney-shaped galls with long, thin telial horns, whereas cedar-hawthorn rust has short, stubby horns and cedar-quince rust forms elongated twig swellings [S1].
