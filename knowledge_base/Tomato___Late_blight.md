## Symptoms
Late blight begins as small, water-soaked areas on leaves that expand into purple-brown, oily-looking blotches [S1]. On the underside of leaves, rings of grayish white mycelium and spore-forming structures may appear around the blotches, and entire leaves can die [S1]. Maryland Extension describes dark, water-soaked leaf and stem lesions that enlarge until the tissue turns brown and dies, with dead leaves often remaining attached [S2]. Under favorable conditions a white fuzzy mold forms on leaf undersides, and stem lesions are brown to nearly black [S2]. Infection spreads rapidly to petioles and young stems [S1]. Infected fruit turn brown but remain firm unless secondary decay organisms invade, and symptoms usually begin on the fruit shoulders [S1]. Fruit lesions may also be shiny, dark or olive-colored and cover large areas, and infected plants can decline and die rapidly [S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
Late blight is caused by Phytophthora infestans, a fungus-like water mold [S1][S2]. It affects all parts of tomato and potato plants [S2]. The pathogen overwinters in potatoes, tomatoes, hairy nightshade and possibly the soil [S1]. The US-23 strain found in the Eastern U.S. in 2009 is an A1 mating type that can only overwinter on live plant tissue, such as potato tubers left in the ground [S2]. Wind easily carries spores to other plants [S1]. Rain spreads spores over short distances and wind over long distances, and the disease can enter gardens on infected transplants and seed potatoes [S2]. Solanaceous weeds such as horsenettle, Eastern black nightshade and jimsonweed can also host the disease [S2].

## Favorable conditions
Prolonged periods of high humidity combined with mild temperatures favor late blight [S1]. With humidity above 90 percent and an average temperature of about 60 to 78 degrees F, infection can occur in roughly 10 hours [S1]. Under ideal conditions the disease spreads rapidly and losses can be severe [S1]. Cool, wet weather favors the disease, which can appear at any time during the growing season [S2]. A return to hot, dry weather slows its development [S2]. Sprinkler irrigation favors the disease, and fungicides are generally needed mainly when it appears during rainy periods or under overhead irrigation [S1]. Late blight rarely occurs in Maryland but is more likely in cooler areas of the state [S2].

## Prevention and cultural control
Where late blight occurs regularly, growers can plant tomato varieties resistant to certain races of the pathogen [S1]. Maryland Extension recommends buying locally grown transplants or starting your own and choosing late blight-resistant varieties [S2]. Transplants should be inspected for late blight before planting [S1]. Volunteer tomato and potato plants and nearby nightshades should be removed, and sprinkler irrigation avoided when possible [S1]. Keeping foliage dry, avoiding overhead watering and avoiding crowded plantings also reduce risk [S2]. Infected plants should be bagged, sealed and left in sunlight for a week before disposal with household trash rather than composted [S2]. Discing tomato fields in fall removes the winter reservoir of the pathogen [S1]. Suspected cases can be reported for monitoring through the USABlight project [S2].

## Chemical and biological control
Protectant fungicides work only if applied before infection, and the disease cannot be cured once symptoms appear [S2]. Once an outbreak occurs, fungicides should be reapplied at regular intervals with thorough coverage [S1]. Maryland Extension names chlorothalonil, mancozeb (for tomatoes) and fixed copper, and notes that copper sprays are acceptable for organic gardening [S2]. UC IPM lists products such as famoxadone plus cymoxanil, dimethomorph, azoxystrobin, azoxystrobin plus difenoconazole, chlorothalonil, mancozeb and pyraclostrobin [S1]. Azoxystrobin should not be applied more than twice in a row before switching to a different mode of action [S1]. Mefenoxam-resistant strains are widespread in California, so that fungicide is no longer effective there [S1]. Product choice and rates must follow the label and local extension recommendations [S1][S2].

## Similar-looking problems
Maryland Extension advises gardeners to learn to distinguish late blight from early blight and Septoria leaf spot, which are common tomato diseases [S2]. Features that point to late blight include oily, purple-brown or dark water-soaked blotches, grayish white growth on leaf undersides, and firm brown fruit lesions starting on the shoulders [S1][S2]. Rapid collapse of plants during cool, wet weather is also characteristic [S1][S2]. If late blight is suspected, close-up photos can be submitted through an extension service such as Ask Extension for confirmation [S2].
