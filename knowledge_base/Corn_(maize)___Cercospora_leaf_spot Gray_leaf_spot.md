## Symptoms
Lesions usually appear first on the lower leaves, about two to four weeks before tasseling or pollination [S1][S3]. Early lesions are tiny, tan to brown spots, sometimes water-soaked, often with a narrow yellow halo that is easiest to see when the leaf is backlit [S2][S3]. Mature lesions are long, narrow and rectangular with parallel sides, because their width is limited by the leaf veins [S1][S2][S3]. They are tan to brown and may turn gray when the fungus produces large numbers of spores on the surface [S1][S2]. Lesions can be up to about 2 inches long and can merge to blight entire leaves [S1][S2]. Lesions may also form on husks and leaf sheaths, and severely affected fields may show stalk rot and lodging [S2][S3].

## Cause and disease cycle
Gray leaf spot is caused by the fungus Cercospora zeae-maydis [S1][S2][S3]. The fungus survives only in infected corn residue left on the soil surface, and it does not survive well when residue is buried [S2][S3]. In spring and summer, spores produced on the residue are carried by wind or splashing rain to lower leaves [S1][S3]. The spores germinate and enter through stomata, and chlorotic symptoms appear about nine days after infection [S3]. Spores produced on mature lesions cause secondary infections that move up the plant and to nearby fields [S2]. The full infection cycle takes about 14 to 21 days on susceptible hybrids [S3]. Susceptibility appears to increase after tasseling, so the disease can intensify late in the season [S2].

## Favorable conditions
Warm, humid weather favors gray leaf spot, especially temperatures in the 80s F with relative humidity above 90 percent for 12 hours or more [S1]. Infection requires at least 12 hours of free moisture on the leaves from dew, fog, rain or irrigation [S2][S3]. Overcast days and prolonged periods of high humidity over two or more days also favor the disease, while temperatures below about 75 F greatly slow its spread [S2]. Continuous corn, minimum tillage, late planting, irrigation, susceptible hybrids and a field history of severe disease all raise the risk [S3]. As little as 10 percent residue cover from a previous corn crop can be enough for an epidemic on a highly susceptible hybrid [S2].

## Prevention and cultural control
Resistant or tolerant hybrids are available, but resistance is quantitative and no hybrid is immune [S1][S2][S3]. Growers should check seed company ratings, keeping in mind that companies may use different rating scales [S3]. Rotating to a non-host crop such as soybean, wheat, oats or alfalfa for one to two years reduces inoculum [S2]. Tillage that buries infected residue reduces the amount of primary inoculum [S1][S2]. Some grasses, including johnsongrass and sorghum, can also host the fungus [S2]. An integrated approach that combines residue management, hybrid selection and fungicides when needed is recommended [S3].

## Chemical and biological control
Foliar fungicides labeled for gray leaf spot are available [S1]. Active ingredients mentioned include systemic materials such as azoxystrobin, pyraclostrobin, trifloxystrobin, propiconazole and tebuconazole, and contact materials such as chlorothalonil and mancozeb [S3]. Products may act preventively, curatively or both [S3]. Treatment decisions are usually made around tasseling, based on the amount of disease on the ear leaf and nearby leaves and on the weather forecast [S3]. Fungicide returns are most likely on susceptible hybrids with high yield potential when several risk factors are present [S3]. Pre-harvest intervals differ among products [S3]. Product choice, rates and timing must follow the product label and local extension recommendations [S3].

## Similar-looking problems
Early gray leaf spot lesions can be confused with anthracnose leaf blight, eyespot and early common rust [S3]. Common rust can be ruled out with magnification, because rust pustules break open to release spores [S3]. Lesions on leaf sheaths are difficult to distinguish from those caused by northern corn leaf blight, southern corn leaf blight and northern corn leaf spot [S2]. To confirm gray leaf spot, look for long, narrow, rectangular, vein-limited tan to gray lesions starting on the lower leaves [S1][S2].
