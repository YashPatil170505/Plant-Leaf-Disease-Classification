## Symptoms
Leaf lesions start as small, pale to dark green, water-soaked spots, often at leaf tips or edges where dew persists [S1][S2]. They expand rapidly into large dark brown or black lesions that often look greasy, and entire leaves can die within days [S1]. Lesions may darken to purplish black and are often surrounded by a yellowish halo [S2][S3]. In humid conditions, white fungal growth appears at lesion edges, mostly on leaf undersides [S1][S3]. Brown to black lesions can also form on stems and petioles [S3]. A heavily infected field has a distinct foul odor [S2]. Tubers show irregular, slightly sunken brown to purplish patches on the skin, with a tan to reddish-brown, dry, granular rot underneath [S1]. This firm brown decay starts at the surface and extends 0.125 to 0.5 inch deep [S3].

## Cause and disease cycle
Late blight is caused by Phytophthora infestans, a fungal-like oomycete [S1]. Potato is the main host, but it also infects tomato, petunia, and hairy nightshade [S1][S2]. The pathogen is an obligate parasite that cannot survive without a living host [S2]. It survives between seasons in infected seed tubers, cull piles, and volunteer plants [S1][S2]. Plants emerging from infected seed produce airborne spores that spread to neighboring plants [S1]. Spores are spread by wind and rain, and the cycle can repeat every 5 to 7 days [S1]. The pathogen can complete many reproductive cycles in one season [S2]. Spores washed into the soil can infect tubers, and tubers can also be infected at harvest through contact with infected foliage or contaminated soil [S1][S2]. Nearby infected potato or tomato plantings can also serve as inoculum sources [S3].

## Favorable conditions
Late blight is favored by free moisture and cool to moderate temperatures [S1]. Night temperatures of 50 to 60 degrees F and day temperatures of 60 to 70 degrees F are most favorable [S1]. Nighttime temperatures in the 50s with daytime temperatures in the 70s, accompanied by rain, fog, or heavy dew, are ideal [S2]. High humidity above 90 percent and average temperatures of 50 to 78 degrees F favor the disease [S3]. Spores need about 12 hours of free moisture to infect [S1]. Overhead sprinkler irrigation can favor the disease under marginal conditions [S3]. In cool, wet weather the disease can spread from leaves to stems and across a field within days [S2]. Hot, dry weather slows disease development [S2].

## Prevention and cultural control
Plant certified, disease-free seed tubers [S1][S2][S3]. Eliminate cull piles and volunteer potatoes, which are key overwintering sources [S1][S2][S3]. Control solanaceous weeds, especially nightshades [S1][S2]. Do not mix seed lots, because seed cutting can transmit late blight [S1]. Limit irrigation, especially at night, and maintain air drainage so foliage dries daily [S1][S3]. Scout fields regularly, especially where blight has occurred before, and monitor nearby tomatoes as possible sources [S1][S2]. Kill vines thoroughly and wait two to three weeks before harvest so skins set [S1][S2][S3]. Minimize bruising and skinning during harvest [S2]. Cultural control alone or chemical control alone will not provide adequate protection during late blight epidemics [S2].

## Chemical and biological control
Where outbreaks are frequent, preventive fungicides are applied when conditions are favorable, with shorter intervals during cool, rainy weather [S3]. Where outbreaks are sporadic, fungicides are applied when lesions appear in the field or in nearby plantings [S3]. Fungicide applications can be based on a forecasting scheme, and once blight is widespread in a field fungicides generally cannot stop it [S2]. Seed-piece treatments labeled for late blight can be used, and phosphorous acid can be applied after harvest to prevent storage infection [S1]. Active ingredients listed for potato late blight include azoxystrobin, chlorothalonil, mancozeb, cymoxanil used with a protectant, dimethomorph, fenamidone, and propamocarb [S3]. Some strains are resistant to mefenoxam, so fungicide groups should be rotated [S1][S3]. Product choice and rates must follow the label and local extension recommendations [S1][S3].

## Similar-looking problems
The fetched sources do not list specific foliar look-alikes, but they note features that help confirm late blight [S1][S2][S3]. Look for rapidly expanding, greasy, dark lesions with white sporulation at their edges on leaf undersides in humid weather [S1][S3]. Other tuber rots, including soft rot bacteria, pink rot, and leak, often colonize late blight lesions and can progress faster, making diagnosis difficult [S1]. Microscopic examination or PCR testing is recommended to confirm late blight [S1]. Confirming the pathogen genotype is also useful, because some strains resist mefenoxam [S1]. Under cool, dry storage, tuber lesions develop slowly as typical dry rots, so storage history should be considered when examining tubers [S2].
