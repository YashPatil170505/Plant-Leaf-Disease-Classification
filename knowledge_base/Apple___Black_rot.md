## Symptoms
On leaves, the disease is called frogeye leaf spot and begins as small purple specks that appear from petal fall onward [S1][S2]. The specks enlarge to spots about 1/8 to 1/4 inch across with a purple margin and a light brown, tan or gray center, giving a frogeye look [S1][S2]. Tiny black fruiting bodies (pycnidia) may form in the centers of older spots [S1][S2]. On fruit, rot usually starts at the calyx end as a single brown spot that enlarges into concentric brown and black rings [S1][S2]. The flesh stays firm and leathery, and the fruit eventually shrivels into a black mummy that may stay on the tree [S1][S2]. On limbs, slightly sunken reddish-brown cankers form and can expand each year, sometimes extending several feet [S1][S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
Black rot is caused by the fungus Botryosphaeria obtusa, also listed under the name Diplodia seriata [S1][S2]. The fungus overwinters in cankers, dead wood, dead bark and twigs, and mummified fruit, and it often colonizes tissue killed by fire blight [S1][S2]. In spring, pycnidia release conidia and perithecia release ascospores, and conidia can continue to be produced during wet periods through the summer [S1][S2]. Spores are spread by splashing rain, wind and insects to leaves, blossoms, young fruit and wounds on wood [S2]. Leaf infection usually occurs around petal fall, when conidia germinate in a film of moisture within 5 to 6 hours and enter through natural openings or wounds [S1][S2]. Fruit infections can begin at bloom or petal fall but usually do not show until midsummer as fruit matures [S1][S2].

## Favorable conditions
Wet weather favors the disease because spores are produced during wet periods, dispersed by rain splash, and require a film of moisture to germinate [S1][S2]. Germination and infection are fastest at about 75 to 80 F [S1]. The disease is especially problematic in warm, humid areas of the eastern United States [S2]. Wounds from insects, hail or growth cracks, as well as open calyx tubes, give the fungus entry points into fruit [S1]. Trees that are stressed or weakened, especially by winter injury, are prone to cankers [S1]. Harvest injuries and harvesting during wet periods increase decay in storage [S2]. Dead shoots killed by fire blight provide favorable sites for the fungus to build up [S2].

## Prevention and cultural control
Sanitation is the primary means of control [S2]. Growers should prune out cankers, dead wood and fire blight-infected shoots, and remove all mummified fruit from trees [S1][S2]. Cankers should be pruned well below the visible margin, and prunings should be removed, buried, burned, or chopped with a flail mower so they decompose quickly [S1][S2]. Controlling fire blight reduces the dead tissue that the black rot fungus colonizes [S2]. Annual pruning and a balanced fertility program based on soil and leaf analysis help keep trees vigorous [S1]. Maintaining tree health and preventing stress reduces canker development [S2].

## Chemical and biological control
Fungicides combined with good sanitation are recommended mainly for the fruit rot phase of the disease [S1]. Captan and strobilurin fungicides (FRAC Group 11) are listed as effective against the fruit rot [S2]. Spray timing affects the level of control, and sanitation remains the main strategy with fungicides as a supplement [S2]. Fungicides are not effective against cankers on weakened trees [S1]. The sources refer growers to regional fruit pest management guides for specific programs [S1]. Product choice, rates and timing must follow the product label and local extension recommendations [S1][S2].

## Similar-looking problems
Frogeye leaf spots can resemble spots caused by spray injury, but the black pycnidia on the upper surface of older spots help tell them apart [S1]. On fruit, black rot can be confused with bitter rot [S2]. Black rot usually produces only one spot per fruit, while bitter rot can produce several, and when cut open the black rot decay is not cone-shaped as bitter rot decay is [S2]. The scaling bark seen on older cankers is not unique to black rot, so it is not a reliable diagnostic sign by itself [S2].
