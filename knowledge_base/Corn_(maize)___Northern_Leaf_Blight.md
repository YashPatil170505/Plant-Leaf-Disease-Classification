## Symptoms
Symptoms usually appear first on the lower leaves and move higher in the canopy as the disease progresses [S1][S2]. Early lesions are long, narrow, tan streaks running parallel to the leaf veins [S1]. These expand into long, elliptical, tan to gray lesions about 1 to 6 inches long that are described as cigar-shaped, which is the diagnostic symptom [S1][S2][S3]. Lesions may have a dark brown margin [S3]. Under moist conditions, dark gray spores form on the lesions, giving them a dirty gray appearance [S2]. Lesions eventually merge and can cover much of the leaf, and severely blighted plants can lose entire leaves [S1][S2]. Lesions can also appear on leaf sheaths and the outer husks of ears, but the kernels are not infected [S1][S2].

## Cause and disease cycle
Northern corn leaf blight is caused by the fungus Exserohilum turcicum, whose sexual stage is Setosphaeria turcica [S1][S2][S3]. Several races of the pathogen exist, and race 1 is the most common [S2]. The fungus overwinters in infected corn residue on the soil surface [S1][S2]. As temperatures rise in spring, it produces spores on the residue that are carried by wind, rain splash or irrigation to the lower leaves of new corn [S1][S3]. Lesions usually form within one to two weeks of infection, and new spores from these lesions spread the disease up the plant and to nearby fields [S1][S3]. At harvest, the pathogen returns to the residue to overwinter [S1].

## Favorable conditions
Moderate temperatures of about 64 to 81 F and extended leaf wetness of more than six hours from rain or dew favor infection [S2][S3]. Spores need roughly 6 to 18 hours of free water on the leaf surface to germinate [S1]. Frequent rainfall, high humidity and long periods of heavy dew favor disease development [S1]. Spores from residue within the field are the main source of inoculum, although spores can also blow in from elsewhere [S3]. Yield losses of up to 30 to 50 percent can occur in dent corn if the disease is established before tasseling [S1].

## Prevention and cultural control
Planting resistant hybrids is the best control, and resistance has been bred into most hybrids [S2][S3]. Resistance can be monogenic, based on single Ht genes that may be overcome by some races, or polygenic, which protects against all races but is less complete [S2]. Growers should check the leaf blight rating of their hybrids with their seed provider, especially in fields with a history of the disease [S1]. Rotating away from corn for one to two years and reducing surface residue through tillage lower the amount of inoculum [S1][S2][S3]. Sorghum and sudangrass should be avoided in rotations because they can serve as alternate hosts [S1].

## Chemical and biological control
Several foliar fungicides are labeled for northern corn leaf blight, and many have good to very good efficacy [S2][S3]. Fungicide use may be profitable if a field is infested and conditions remain favorable [S1]. Applications between the VT (tasseling) and R3 growth stages have shown the greatest efficacy [S1]. Susceptible hybrids may benefit from a fungicide when the disease begins developing around tasseling [S3]. Regional fungicide efficacy tables can help in choosing a product [S3]. Product choice, rates and timing must follow the product label and local extension recommendations [S1][S3].

## Similar-looking problems
Gray leaf spot is a similar leaf disease, but it favors warmer temperatures than northern corn leaf blight [S1]. Gray leaf spot lesions are narrow and rectangular and limited by leaf veins, while northern corn leaf blight lesions are longer, elliptical and cigar-shaped [S2][S4]. Lesions on leaf sheaths caused by gray leaf spot, northern corn leaf blight and other leaf blights can be difficult to tell apart [S4]. On hybrids carrying Ht resistance genes, northern corn leaf blight lesions are smaller and chlorotic, may form linear streaks and rarely produce spores, so they can look unlike typical lesions [S2].
